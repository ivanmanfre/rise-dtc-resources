How to read it

Why two brands spending the same amount can post very different CAC

An apparel brand running a size run from XS to 3XL sees a wider spread of guest checkouts, duplicate accounts and refunded first orders than almost any other category, and every one of those artifacts moves the new customer count before it ever reaches this calculator. Two brands can spend the identical forty thousand dollars in a month and post very different blended CAC, not because one bought cheaper clicks, but because one of them counted a customer whose only order came back as a refund. That single exclusion, applied or skipped, moves the denominator enough to change the answer.

This page keeps three CAC numbers separate on purpose. Blended CAC divides every dollar spent, media plus agency retainers plus creative plus affiliate fees, by new customers. Media-only CAC divides only the media line by the same customers, which reads cheaper because it leaves the fixed costs sitting around the media buy out of the count. Platform-reported CAC divides your media spend by the conversions the ad platform's own dashboard credits itself, on its own attribution window. All three are correct arithmetic. Only blended CAC is the number your bank account agrees with, which is why it is the headline on this page and the other two sit underneath it as context.

Why CAC carries no returns line

Every other calculator in this set spends a paragraph on what happens to a returned order. This one does not, on purpose. You paid the ad, the agency retainer and the affiliate fee the moment you spent them, and whether the customer's first order later comes back changes nothing about what you already paid to acquire that person. Returns enter this page in exactly one place: the new customer count itself. A shopper whose only order was fully refunded never became a customer, and the field above tells you to leave that person out. Get that exclusion wrong and every CAC figure on this page reads too low, quietly, with no error message to catch it.

What the channel split is actually for

Channel A and Channel B are meant to be read as pieces of the totals above them, not as a separate calculation. Enter what each channel spent and how many new customers it is credited with, and the difference between the total and the two channels becomes the unattributed bucket: organic search, direct traffic, referral, or a channel you have not split out yet. On an apparel brand with a strong repeat customer base, that unattributed share is often the cheapest acquisition you have, because it is customers finding the brand without a click being paid for. When the two channels alone add up to more spend or more customers than the totals you entered, the page flags it as a data error rather than quietly turning the unattributed line negative and moving on.

Why the attribution gap is a definition problem

Ad platforms attribute a conversion inside a window they define, often crediting a view or a click from several days before the purchase, and two platforms will both claim the same sale if both ran an ad the shopper saw. Your store counts the same sale exactly once. Neither number is wrong. They are measuring different things, and the overstatement factor on this page exists to put a figure on the size of that difference for your own account rather than leaving it as a vague sense that the ads dashboard looks too good. There is no publicly verified figure for a typical apparel CAC, for the size of the post-attribution-change gap across brands, or for the organic share of new customers that met our sourcing bar, so this page prints none of the three. Every number here comes from what you enter.

Reading the range instead of the point estimate

The spread above flexes only the new customer count, twenty percent lower and twenty percent higher, and holds spend fixed. That choice is deliberate: spend is a fact, cleared through your card processor or your ad account ledger, while the customer count is where guest checkout duplicates and refund timing create genuine room to be wrong. Your real CAC sits somewhere in that range. The point estimate in the middle is the least useful number on this page, useful mainly as the midpoint of a range you should actually be reading.

What CAC leaves unanswered

A blended CAC tells you what a customer cost. Whether the acquisition made money depends on what that customer is worth, after returns, product cost, shipping and card fees are counted on the order that bought them, and that is a separate question this page does not answer. Answering it needs the CAC figure above alongside a contribution margin figure from elsewhere in this set. Run the number here next to true profit per order and break-even ROAS before deciding whether the acquisition cost is a problem or a strength.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this math

Why is my blended CAC higher than what the ad platform reports?
Ad platforms report the conversions their own attribution window credits to a click or a view, often extending several days past the purchase and crediting touches other platforms also claim. Blended CAC divides everything you spent by the new customers your store actually recorded, with no overlap and no attribution window. The gap between the two numbers is the platform overstatement, and this calculator prints it as a factor so you can see exactly how much cheaper the platform claims you are.
Should I budget off blended CAC or media-only CAC?
Blended CAC, because it is the number your bank account agrees with. Media-only CAC leaves out agency retainers, creative production, affiliate fees and acquisition tooling, so it always reads lower than the true cost of a customer. Media-only CAC still has a use: it isolates what the media buy itself is doing, separate from the fixed costs sitting around it.
What counts as a new customer in this calculator?
A first-time buyer your store recorded in the period, with fully refunded first orders removed. A customer whose only order came back is not a customer yet, and leaving that order in the count quietly lowers your CAC by counting an acquisition that never landed. Pull the count from your store, not from an ad platform's conversion tally.
My channel spend and channel customers do not add up to my totals. What does that mean?
Channel A and Channel B are meant to be a subset of the totals you entered above them, with whatever is left over counted as unattributed: organic, direct, referral, or a channel you have not split out yet. When the two channels alone add up to more spend or more customers than your total, one of the numbers is wrong, and this calculator flags it rather than quietly absorbing the difference into a negative unattributed line.
Why does the range only move the customer count and not the spend?
Spend is the one number in this calculator that is a fact: it is what cleared your card or your ad account ledger. The new customer count is where guest checkout duplicates, attribution windows and refunded first orders create room to be wrong, so the range flexes that count twenty percent in each direction and holds spend fixed. Your real CAC sits between the low and high figures, and the point estimate in the middle is the least useful number on this page.
Does a low CAC mean the acquisition is profitable?
CAC alone does not confirm profitability, because it measures what a customer cost to acquire and says nothing about what that customer is worth once returns, product cost, shipping and card fees are counted. A CAC that looks cheap can still sit on an order that loses money if the contribution margin behind it is thin. Run this number next to true profit per order and break-even ROAS before you read it as good news.
