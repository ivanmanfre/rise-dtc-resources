How to read it

Why a discount needs more lift than the sale rack suggests

The version of this math most promo decks run is simple almost to the point of being wrong: you gave away a discount worth so many cents on the dollar, so you need enough extra orders to cover the cents. That version assumes every discounted order gets kept. In apparel, it almost never fully is. A promo buyer who would not have bought at full price is, by definition, a more marginal purchase, and marginal purchases come back more often. The gap between what the sale rack math promises and what the order actually leaves you is the entire reason this page exists.

Sizing drives most of it. A promo shopper who is already unsure whether a garment fits is more willing to order it anyway once the price drops, plan to keep whichever size works, and return the other. That behavior was going to happen at full price too, but a lower price lowers the bar for taking the gamble in the first place. The order volume the promo bought includes some share of orders that were only ever going to be tried on, not kept, and the naive math counts every one of them as revenue.

Two return rates, on purpose

This calculator asks for the full-price return rate and the discounted return rate separately, because folding them into one blended figure hides exactly the gap that matters. The default sets the discounted rate a few points above baseline, which is a starting assumption to edit, not a published finding. There is no benchmark dataset that reports the split between full-price and promo-driven returns, which is why the published figures further down this page cover general return rates only. Pull your own split if you can. If you cannot, set the two rates equal and treat the required-lift numbers as a floor rather than the real number.

What the all-in return cost bundles

The all-in return cost is one field carrying four costs: the return label, the inspection and repack labor, the outbound shipping you already paid and cannot get back, and the card processing fee the processor keeps on a refund. Bundling them keeps the tool at eight fields instead of twelve, at the price of losing the individual breakdown. The model also assumes the returned unit itself is resellable, meaning the product cost comes home with it. If a meaningful share of your promo returns are worn, out of season, or otherwise unsellable at full price, that cost is not modelled separately here. Raise the all-in return cost to cover it, or expect your real number to run worse than this one.

The real required lift versus the returns-blind one

The returns-blind required lift is the ratio of the discount to what is left of the margin after the discount: discount divided by margin minus discount. It answers a narrower question than it looks like it does. It tells you how many more orders would offset the margin given away if every single one of those orders got kept. The real required lift answers a different question: how many more orders does it take once each one carries its own chance of coming back, refunding the discounted price, and adding a handling cost on top. Every additional discounted order carries a return risk of its own, and that risk compounds as volume grows rather than staying fixed. The required-lift table on this page prints both columns at four discount depths, ten, fifteen, twenty and twenty-five percent, so you can see the two numbers pull apart as the discount gets deeper. At a shallow discount the gap is modest. Past a certain depth, the real number can run many multiples of the returns-blind one, because the margin cushion the promo has left to absorb returns shrinks faster than the discount itself grows.

When no lift saves the discount

There is a point where the discount alone exceeds the contribution margin at full price. Once that happens, a single discounted order that gets kept already loses money before a single return is counted, and returns can only make it worse. No amount of extra volume repairs that, because more orders at a negative contribution multiply the loss rather than diluting it. When this page shows that no lift saves the discount, the fix is a shallower discount, a higher-margin product, or a more targeted return-rate reduction on that specific line.

The lift you enter is the guess

Expected order lift is the one number on this page that nobody measures before the promo runs. It is a forecast, usually built from the last promo that felt similar. The honest spread above recomputes the same window with that forecast flexed twenty percent lower and twenty percent higher, so you can see whether the verdict survives being wrong about the one input you cannot check in advance. When the whole spread sits on the same side of zero, the lift guess is not what decides the outcome, the underlying order economics are. When the spread straddles zero, the promo's fate rides entirely on a number nobody has measured yet.

How to use this before you launch a promo

Run your actual numbers, not the defaults, before you commit spend. If the verdict says the promo pays but only barely, treat the order count you enter as a floor to hit rather than a target to aim for, since a small miss on lift or a slightly higher return rate than expected can flip the sign. If it says no lift saves the discount, the conversation with whoever wants to run it should be about the discount depth or the margin, not about pushing harder on volume. And whichever way it comes out, the required-lift table is the one line worth bringing into that conversation, because it is the single clearest way to show a stakeholder why the sale-rack math and the real math disagree.
