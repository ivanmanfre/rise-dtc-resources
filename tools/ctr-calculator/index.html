  • Pooled two-proportion z-test, normal approximation to the binomial. No continuity correction, two-sided.
  • Built for exactly two variants. Running six ad sets through the same pairwise test invites a false positive; each additional pair needs its own correction, which this page does not attempt.
  • The confidence interval on each rate is a fixed 95% Wald interval, independent of the confidence level chosen for the significance test itself, and the label says so.
  • Fewer than 10 clicks or 10 non-clicks in a variant makes the normal approximation unreliable at that count, and the panel prints a warning instead of a false read.
  • Placement and creative format are labels only. Paid impressions and email sends are different denominators and never share a benchmark row.
  • Sample size is the count each variant still needs to call the gap you are currently seeing at 80% statistical power, not a guarantee either variant will hold its current rate once it gets there.

Rounding happens at display time only. All the arithmetic runs in full precision, so nothing drifts between the panel, the bar above and the rows here.

How to read it

Why a bigger lift can still be noise

Click-through rate is the easiest number in ecommerce advertising to misread, because it looks like a fact and behaves like an estimate. Variant B reading 1.24% against variant A's 1.00% is not a law of the universe, it is one sample of clicks out of one sample of impressions, and a second week of the same test would print a slightly different pair of numbers even if nothing about the creative changed. The question this page answers is not "which number is bigger", it is "how much of that gap is the creative, and how much of it is the sample".

Apparel accounts feel this hardest, because apparel ad accounts run more creative than almost any other vertical. A new color drop, a new model, a new fit shot, each one earns its own test, and each test runs on a smaller slice of the account's total traffic than the last one. A 24% lift on 50,000 impressions per variant is a different claim than a 30% lift on 2,000 impressions per variant, even though the second number looks more exciting on a slide. The test below is what tells the two apart.

What the two-proportion test is actually doing

Both variants are being read as a rate: clicks divided by impressions. The test pools both variants into one combined rate, on the theory that if the creatives performed identically, that pooled rate is the one number both of them should be drawing from. It then asks how far apart variant A and variant B would need to be, in units of their own sampling noise, before that pooled-rate theory becomes hard to believe. That distance is the z-score. The p-value converts the z-score into a plain question: if the two creatives were truly identical, how often would chance alone produce a gap this size or larger. A small p-value means chance is a poor explanation. It does not mean the true gap is exactly the one you measured, only that a gap of roughly this size is very unlikely to be nothing.

None of this needs a continuity correction or a one-sided test to be honest. This page runs the two-sided version, because a creative you expected to win can still lose, and a test that only checks for wins in the expected direction will occasionally call a loss a win. It also runs the pooled-variance version rather than the unpooled one, which is the standard choice when the null hypothesis being tested is that the two rates are equal.

Why confidence level and the confidence interval are two different things

The confidence level you pick above sets the bar the significance test has to clear. The confidence interval printed under each rate is a separate, fixed 95% Wald interval, on purpose. Moving the significance bar to 90% or 99% should change whether you call the gap real. It should not change how uncertain either individual rate is, and pinning the interval keeps the two questions from blurring into one.

The line most people skip: sample size

A gap can be real and still too early to spend a media budget on. The sample size row on this page answers a forward-looking question: given the gap you are currently seeing, how many impressions would each variant need before that gap clears your chosen bar with reasonable statistical power. When your actual impression counts sit well below that number, a NOT YET verdict is not a failure of the creative, it is an honest description of not having run the test long enough yet. Below 10 clicks or 10 non-clicks in either variant, the panel adds a separate warning, because the normal approximation this test relies on stops being trustworthy at that count no matter what the p-value prints.

Why this page never turns a click into a dollar

A click-through rate carries no return rate, no card fee and no acquisition cost, because none of those apply yet. The customer has not bought anything. Folding a CTR gap straight into a revenue projection is where blended benchmark pages tend to mislead, especially ones that average paid social, paid search and email into one CTR column as if the three shared a denominator. They do not: a feed impression, a search impression and an email open are three different events, counted three different ways, and stacking them into one number erases the placement difference that made the comparison worth running in the first place. This page keeps that door deliberately closed. Once you have a rate you trust, the question of what a click is worth belongs to a calculator built for money, not for rates, which is why cpc-calculator and cpa-calculator sit in the cross-links below rather than inside this page's math.

Read the verdict, read the bar it had to clear, and read the sample size before deciding a creative test is over. On apparel accounts running several tests a week, that third number is usually the one that saves the budget.

Questions we get

Five things founders ask about this math

Why does a bigger lift sometimes read as not significant?
Significance depends on the size of the gap relative to the size of the sample, not on the size of the gap alone. A 30% lift measured on 2,000 impressions per variant carries far more sampling noise than a 24% lift measured on 50,000 impressions per variant, so the smaller-looking lift can clear the bar while the bigger-looking one does not. The p-value is what separates the two, and the sample size row shows how much more data the thinner test still needs.
What does "not significant yet" mean, and can I still act on variant B?
It means the gap you are seeing has not cleared the bar you set, which is a verdict about the evidence so far rather than about variant B. The honest move is to keep the test running toward the sample size this page prints, then read the verdict again rather than calling the winner off a partial sample.
Why do you print a sample size instead of just a yes or no?
A yes or no with no forward path leaves you guessing whether to keep the test running. The sample size row answers that directly: it is the number of impressions each variant would need, at your chosen confidence level and 80% statistical power, to call a gap the size you are currently seeing.
Why doesn't this page turn CTR into revenue?
A click has no return rate, no card fee and no acquisition cost attached to it yet, because no purchase has happened. Blending click-through rate into a revenue figure is exactly where combined paid-and-email benchmark pages go wrong, since a feed impression, a search impression and an email open are different denominators. Once a rate has cleared this test, cpc-calculator and cpa-calculator are built to answer what a click or an order is actually worth.
What if I only have one creative running, with no variant B yet?
Turn the second-creative toggle off. The panel reads variant A on its own, prints its rate and its 95% interval, and hides the comparison math until a second creative is entered.
