  • Returned goods that go back to full-price stock will sell again at the same price, so their revenue is deferred rather than destroyed. That is why the resellable share sits inside the kept factor, and why a 100% share collapses the adjustment to nothing.
  • This is a revenue view. Return shipping, restocking labor and the markdown on units that cannot be resold are costs, so they live in True Profit Per Order and MER Calculator instead.
  • Total ad spend is every paid channel, which makes blended ROAS on this page the same arithmetic as MER. The page states the equivalence and prints one number.

How to read it

One ratio, and the two questions it hides

Blended ROAS on this page is total revenue divided by total ad spend. Every channel sits in the numerator, every paid channel sits in the denominator, and no attribution model touches either one. That is the same arithmetic marketing efficiency ratio describes, so the two names point at one number here, and the page says so. It is a useful number precisely because it is hard to argue with: your bank statement produced the denominator and your sales dashboard produced the numerator.

What it hides is composition and refunds. The numerator counts revenue that was booked, including the orders a customer sends back three weeks later, and it counts revenue that arrived through email, through organic search and through people who typed the domain in directly. A ratio built that way can move for reasons that have nothing to do with the ad account. A strong email month lifts it. A refund wave two weeks after a launch drops it. This page separates both effects out and prints them next to the headline instead of leaving them inside it.

Where the returns adjustment comes from

Refunds are handled with one factor. Take the return rate, multiply by the share of returned goods that cannot go back to full price, and subtract the result from one. That is the share of booked revenue that is never given back and never written off, and multiplying it through gives the kept revenue behind the ratio. Goods that do return to full-price stock will sell again at the same price, so their revenue is deferred rather than destroyed, which is why the resellable share sits inside the factor. At a 100% resellable share the factor is exactly one, the adjustment contributes nothing, and the returns-adjusted ratio lands exactly on the raw one. That collapse is deliberate, and it is the same one True Profit Per Order uses.

The difference between the two ratios gets a line of its own, printed in x rather than as a percentage, because that is the unit an operator already thinks in. On the numbers this page opens with, a store books a 5.00x blend and keeps 4.25x of it, so refunds cost that account three quarters of a point of ROAS before any optimization was ever attempted.

Paid dependency, and when the blend stops carrying information

The second question is how much of the revenue behind the ratio the ads are actually carrying. Enter the revenue you attribute to paid and the page prints it as a share of the total. When that share is low, the blended number is telling you something the ad account never could: other channels are carrying real weight, and the ad spend is being measured against a business that partly runs without it. When the share climbs, the blended number converges on the paid number, and the two stop being independent readings of anything.

This tool draws that line at 70%, and that threshold is a judgment call made here rather than a figure quoted from a publisher. No source states a paid-dependency benchmark for DTC, so the page labels its own line as its own line. Above it, the verdict says paid carries the store, and the reading to take is that the blend has stopped adding information on top of what the platform dashboard was already reporting.

When the paid revenue row is left empty, the dependency step is skipped entirely and the page asks for the number instead of assuming a zero. An empty field is an unknown, and printing 0% paid would be a claim you never made. The same rule governs the organic and direct row: it is derived as the remainder only once one of the two rows above it is filled, and its label then absorbs anything else you left out.

What the ad platforms claim

Switch on the platform ROAS row and the page multiplies it by the same ad spend to get the revenue your platforms are claiming, then measures that claim two ways: against the whole store's blended ratio, and against your own paid ROAS. The second comparison is the one worth watching, since both sides of it are measuring the same spend against the same intended slice of revenue. When the platforms' claimed revenue exceeds what the store booked, a separate chip appears next to the verdict. It never moves the tier. A tier is a money verdict and an attribution gap is a measurement problem, so they get separate chips and separate readings.

What this page deliberately does not print

There is no break-even floor anywhere on this page. A floor comes out of your contribution margin after returns, and that calculation belongs to MER Calculator, which prices it from the margin you enter there. Costs are absent for the same reason: return shipping, restocking labor and the markdown on units that cannot be resold are real, and they live in True Profit Per Order, which carries them per order. This page stays a revenue view on purpose, so that every number on it can be traced back to two figures your accounting already agrees on.

Three dials move the answer once you have it. The return rate, which sizing guidance and product photography both touch upstream of any ad decision. The resellable share, which is a returns-grading question. And the split between paid and everything else, which is the slowest of the three to move and the one that decides how much the blended ratio was ever worth reading.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this math

Is blended ROAS the same thing as MER?
On this page, yes. Total revenue over total ad spend is the arithmetic both names describe, so the tool states the equivalence and moves on to the two things the single ratio leaves out: what refunds take off it, and how much of the revenue behind it your ads are carrying. MER Calculator answers the other question, which is what ratio your margin requires before the spend pays for itself.
Why does a ROAS calculator ask for a return rate?
Because the numerator is booked revenue, and a share of booked revenue goes back to the customer. The returns-adjusted ratio recomputes the same division on the revenue you keep, and the gap between the two is printed in x so you can see what refunds cost the headline. Enter a zero return rate and the two ratios sit on top of each other, which is exactly what should happen.
What is the resellable share doing inside a revenue ratio?
A refund removes revenue whatever happens to the goods, but a returned unit that goes straight back into full-price stock will sell again at the same price, so that revenue is deferred to a later period rather than destroyed. Treating it that way is what makes the adjustment collapse exactly at a 100% resellable share, and it keeps this page consistent with True Profit Per Order. Leave the row switched off and the page treats the share as none and prints that alongside the adjusted number.
What blended ROAS should I be aiming for?
This page publishes no target, because a blended-ROAS floor that holds independently of margin does not exist. Your floor comes from your contribution margin after returns, and MER Calculator computes it from the margin and return rate you enter there. The published figures quoted further up this page are context for what other stores report, and they are not a threshold to clear.
My channel rows add up to more than my total revenue. What happened?
Usually double-counted attribution: an order that email and paid both claim gets counted once in each row. The page prints the overflow in dollars and leaves your entries exactly as you typed them, since rescaling them silently would hide the contradiction. Paid dependency is held at 100% for display when it computes above that, while the verdict reads the unclamped figure, so a store at 120% still trips the tier it should.
My platforms report a higher ROAS than the store produced. Which one is right?
Both are measuring what they were built to measure. The platforms count conversions their models attribute to their own ads, with view-through windows and cross-device matching inside the number. The store counts revenue that landed in the bank. When the platforms' claimed revenue exceeds what the store booked, the page raises a separate chip next to the verdict and leaves the tier alone, because the money verdict and the measurement warning are two different readings and collapsing them into one chip would lose both.
