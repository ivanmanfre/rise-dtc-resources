How to read it

What returns take out of your quoted margin

Every pricing conversation starts with the same two numbers: markup and gross margin. Markup asks how much you charged over cost. Gross margin asks what share of the price is profit before anything else happens to the order. Both are honest answers to a narrow question, and both are calculated the same way whether the product ships once and stays sold or ships, comes back, and ships again to someone else. That is Section A and Section B on this page, and they are deliberately returns-blind, so you can see the exact size of the gap once Section C removes that assumption.

Apparel is where the gap is widest. A shopper orders two sizes to keep one. A garment comes back worn once, or out of season, or without its tags. None of that shows up in a markup calculation, because markup only ever looks at one clean sale. It shows up the moment you ask what a unit actually banks once the returns that follow it are counted, and that is the entire reason a third section exists on this page.

The four places returns enter Section C

Returns touch the arithmetic four separate times, and this page keeps all four visible instead of folding them into one blended number. First, revenue kept: only the share of units that stay sold leaves money behind, so revenue is multiplied by one minus the return rate. Second, product cost, net of resale: you recover the landed cost only on the resellable share, so a unit that comes back unsellable charges you for the goods twice. Third, fulfilment: shipping, pick and pack, and payment fees are charged on every unit shipped, whether it stays sold or not, because that cost clears before the customer decides. Fourth, return handling: the label, the inspection and the repack, counted on the expected share of units that come back.

Put together, realized profit per unit is what one unit sold actually leaves you once all four are counted. Realized margin, net of returns, measures that profit against the revenue you actually keep. Realized margin, on sticker price, measures the same profit against the price on the tag, so it lines up directly against the gross margin figure from Section B. The distance between those two margins is the margin gap, and it is the single most honest number a pricing page can print: it is the difference between the margin you quote in a deck and the margin that lands in the bank.

What resellable share is actually doing

Resellable share is the input most pricing spreadsheets skip, and it is the one that moves the answer most. At 80% resellable, one unit in five that comes back cannot go straight back on the shelf at full price. It gets marked down, moved to an outlet channel, or written off, and the landed cost on that unit is gone twice over: once when you bought it, once when you could not sell it at the price you planned. Drag that dial from 80% down to 50% and watch the realized margin move while the gross margin above it does not move at all, because gross margin never asked the question in the first place.

Two target prices, on purpose

Most pricing tools solve a single equation: cost over one minus target margin, which answers what price clears your target margin if every unit sold stays sold. This page always calculates that number, labeled target price, returns-blind, and it always also calculates a second number that solves the same target margin after revenue lost to returns, product cost carried twice, fulfilment and handling are all subtracted first. That second number is the target price, net of returns, and it is almost always the higher of the two, because it has more cost to recover from fewer kept units. The gap between them, what this page calls the price gap, is the plainest possible statement of what returns add to your sticker price. The toggle on the input side decides which of the two numbers becomes the headline you see first, and both stay calculated either way.

When a target price sits below your current price

Sometimes the net-of-returns target price comes back lower than what you already charge. That means the margin you are asking for is already being met or beaten at your current price, once returns are counted honestly, and the page says exactly that instead of implying a price cut. Other times the net-of-returns target sits well above your current price, which means hitting that margin needs either a higher sticker price, a lower return rate, a higher resellable share, or some combination of the three. The page shows you the size of the gap so the conversation about closing it starts from a real number instead of a guess.

Reading the range

Return rate is the one input on this page most operators are estimating rather than measuring, so it is the one flexed twenty percent in each direction to produce the honest spread above and the target-price range printed in the math. When the whole spread sits close together, the return-rate guess is not doing much work and the price and margin you see are close to load bearing. When it spreads wide, the number you should trust least is the one you are least sure about, and it is worth pulling a real return rate off your own order data before repricing anything.

Questions we get

Five things founders ask about this math

Why do Section A and Section B ignore returns completely?
Because markup and gross margin are the two numbers most pricing conversations already run on, and they are always calculated as a single clean sale, with no return in the picture. Keeping them returns-blind here means the gap that shows up once Section C adds returns back in is the honest size of the gap, not an artifact of a different formula.
What does resellable at full price actually change?
It sets how much of the landed product cost comes back to you on a returned unit. At 100% resellable, every returned unit goes straight back on the shelf and the cost is fully recovered. Below that, the unresellable share gets marked down, moved to outlet, or written off, and you are carrying that product cost twice: once on the original sale and once on the unit that cannot be resold at the price you planned.
Why are there two target prices, and which one should I use?
Target price, returns-blind, is the classic cost over one minus margin answer. Target price, net of returns, solves the same target margin after revenue lost to returns, product cost carried twice, fulfilment and return handling are all subtracted first. Both are always calculated. The toggle only decides which one is shown as the headline. If your margin target needs to survive contact with your real return rate, use the net of returns figure.
What does the margin gap actually mean?
It is the distance, in percentage points, between the gross margin you would quote from Section B and the realized margin, on sticker price, from Section C. It is the plainest way to see how much of the margin you think you are running is actually being taken by returns, product cost you do not recover, fulfilment and handling before a single dollar reaches the bank.
What does it mean if the target price, net of returns, comes back below my current price?
It means the margin you are targeting is already being met or beaten at the price you already charge, once returns are counted honestly. No price change is implied by that result, and none is suggested. It is worth checking the other direction too: if the target price sits well above your current price, closing that gap needs a higher sticker price, a lower return rate, a higher resellable share, or some mix of the three.
