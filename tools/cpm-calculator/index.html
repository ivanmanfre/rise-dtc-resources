The math

Every line, with your numbers written into it

These rows update as you move the dials above or switch modes.

  • CPM, spend and impressions convert one way, exactly. Neither conversion mode carries a returns assumption, because neither one makes a profit claim.
  • Affordable CPM is a bid ceiling. It uses your contribution margin and your return rate to say what one impression can be worth, nothing more.
  • Impressions to clicks to orders is a clean multiplicative funnel with no view-through credit added on top.
  • The click-through rate and the conversion rate are the two guessed inputs in affordable CPM mode. Because the ceiling is linear in both, flexing each 20% gives an exact low and high, not a simulation.
  • Break-even means the impression cost exactly equals the contribution it produces. It leaves nothing over for overhead.

Rounding happens at display time only. All the arithmetic runs in full precision, so nothing drifts between the panel, the spread and the rows above.

How to read it

Why a CPM number alone never tells you if an order is profitable

CPM measures one thing: what it costs to put an ad in front of 1,000 people. It says nothing about how many of those people click, how many of the clicks turn into an order, what the order is worth, or how many of those orders come back. Two brands can pay exactly the median CPM quoted in the panel above, Triple Whale's 2025 figure of $14.19, and land in completely different places, because the funnel underneath it, and the return rate sitting inside that funnel, are doing all the real work. That gap is why this page ships in two halves. The first three modes are pure conversion: spend, impressions and CPM, moved between each other with no assumption attached. The fourth mode, affordable CPM, is the one that turns a funnel and a return rate into a ceiling you can actually bid against.

The three conversion modes exist because the same three numbers get reported in three different shapes depending on where you are reading them. A platform dashboard reports spend and impressions and makes you divide. A media plan quotes a CPM and a budget and makes you multiply. A reach forecast quotes a CPM and a budget and asks how many impressions that buys. None of those three questions carries a profit claim, so none of them touch returns, contribution margin or anything about the product. They are unit conversions, and the calculator treats them as exactly that, both directions checked against each other so a typo shows up as a failed round trip rather than a silent error.

Where returns actually enter the arithmetic

Returns only show up once you ask a money question, and the only money question this page asks is affordable CPM. The logic runs backward from the order. Start with the contribution margin the order carries before anything comes back, the share of the order price left after product cost, shipping and processing. Multiply that by the share of orders that stick, one minus the return rate. Then subtract the net cost of the orders that do not stick, the label, the inspection and the markdown, as a share of AOV, multiplied by the return rate again. What is left is the returns-adjusted contribution one order actually delivers. Multiply that by the number of orders 1,000 impressions produce, impressions to clicks by the click-through rate, clicks to orders by the landing page conversion rate, and the result is the highest CPM that funnel can pay and still break even. Pay more than that number and the impression costs more than the order is worth before you have covered a single dollar of overhead.

Apparel is the case where this matters most, because apparel return rates run well above the cross-category average and the two ad platforms most apparel brands run on price impressions very differently. TikTok's platform-wide median CPM sits close to Meta's, but the apparel-specific row on TikTok drops to a fraction of that median, reflecting a channel where apparel competition for attention is lighter than the platform average. Meanwhile Triple Whale's full-year 2025 read, quoted in the panel above, has Meta CPM climbing across every industry it tracks, roughly 20% year over year. Put those two facts together and a brand comparing its Meta bid against its TikTok bid on CPM alone is comparing a number that keeps rising against a number that reflects a comparatively uncrowded niche, neither of which says anything about whether either channel's orders are worth what they cost once the return rate is counted.

The two guessed inputs, and why the spread is linear

Affordable CPM asks for two numbers nobody has measured yet for a new placement: the click-through rate and the landing page conversion rate. Both get flexed 20% worse and 20% better, which multiplies out to 0.64 and 1.44 of the midpoint rather than the more familiar looking 0.8 and 1.2, because the ceiling is the product of both rates together. That multiplication is exact, not simulated, so the pessimistic and optimistic figures printed on this page are the real bounds of what a 20% miss on either rate does to the number, not an approximation.

What the ceiling does and does not tell you

When the ceiling comes back at zero, the message on the page says so plainly: the order loses money before the impression is even paid for, and no CPM is affordable until the contribution margin, the return rate or the return cost changes. When the ceiling comes back positive, treat it as the most a specific placement can cost before it stops paying for itself, rather than a target to spend down to. A campaign bidding well under its own ceiling still has to clear a minimum reach to be worth running at all, and this calculator has nothing to say about that. What it does say, reliably, is the one number every CPM comparison across channels is missing: the price a channel actually has to beat, given your product's margin and your customers' habit of sending things back.

Questions we get

Five things founders ask about this math

Why doesn't the CPM I'm quoted tell me if the campaign is profitable?
CPM only measures what it costs to reach 1,000 people. It has no view of your click-through rate, your conversion rate, your order value or your return rate, and any of those four can turn an identical CPM into a profitable campaign for one brand and a losing one for another. Affordable CPM mode exists to close that gap by running your own funnel and your own return rate backward into a bid ceiling.
What does "affordable CPM" actually mean?
It is the highest price you can pay per 1,000 impressions and still exactly break even, once the impression turns into a click, the click turns into an order, and the return rate and return cost take their share of that order's contribution margin. Bid below it and the campaign has room to also cover overhead. Bid above it and the media cost alone outweighs what the order was worth.
Why is TikTok's apparel CPM so much lower than Meta's?
Published 2025 data puts TikTok's apparel and accessories CPM well below TikTok's own platform-wide median, while Meta does not break its CPM out by apparel at all in the same report. It reflects how crowded a channel is for a given vertical at a given time, not a permanent rule, and it is a reason to check affordable CPM per channel rather than assume one platform is cheaper across the board.
Why do CPMs keep going up?
Triple Whale's full-year 2025 benchmark, quoted with its link in the published figures panel on this page, shows Meta CPM rising across every industry it tracks, roughly 20% year over year. That trend is exactly why a fixed affordable CPM ceiling is worth checking on a schedule rather than once: the funnel that clears it this quarter may not clear it once the median CPM has climbed again.
My click-through rate and conversion rate are both guesses for a new placement. Does that break the ceiling?
It widens it, which is why the spread is printed above. Both rates are flexed 20% worse and 20% better, which multiplies to a 0.64x to 1.44x band around the midpoint because the ceiling is the product of both. If the whole band sits comfortably above zero, a 20% miss on either guess still leaves room. If it dips toward zero, get a real click-through rate and conversion rate from a small test before committing real budget.
