How to read it

Why gross margin and returns-adjusted margin are different numbers

Gross margin is the number most spreadsheets show: price minus product cost minus shipping minus the small variable costs, all counted once, on every unit as if every unit stays sold. It is a clean number and it is the wrong number for a returns business, because a share of those units come back, and coming back does not refund the costs the way it refunds the revenue. Contribution margin, the returns-adjusted version, keeps the same cost inputs and changes only how it treats the units that return. The gap between the two rows on this page is the cost of returns, priced in dollars per unit instead of left as a sentence in a board deck.

Apparel is the hard case, and it is the case these defaults are built around. A shopper who orders two sizes to keep one has bought two units and paid for one. The gross margin line has no way to see that. It assumes the sale that happened is the sale that stays. The returns-adjusted line asks four separate questions about every unit shipped, and this page keeps all four visible instead of folding them into one estimate.

The four places returns enter the arithmetic

First, refunded revenue: only the kept share of units leaves money behind, so revenue is multiplied by one minus the return rate. Second, product cost: you recover inventory value only on the resellable share, so a return that cannot be resold at full price charges you for the goods a second time. Third, return handling: the label, the inspection and the repack, counted on the expected share of units that come back. Fourth, everything that stays fixed: outbound shipping and the other variable costs are charged on every unit shipped and never credited back, because the carrier and the processor have already been paid whether or not the customer keeps the order.

Notice what does not change between the two margin rows. Product cost as a dollar figure, shipping and the other variable costs are the same inputs in both calculations. The only difference is that the returns-adjusted row multiplies revenue by the kept share, nets product cost against the resellable share, and adds the handling cost of the returns you expect. Two calculators fed the same nine numbers should never disagree on gross margin. They will disagree on contribution margin the moment one of them ignores returns.

Resellable share is the dial most people skip

When a garment comes back worn, unfolded, without tags, or in a season that has already turned over, it stops being A-grade inventory. It moves to markdown, to an outlet channel, or to a write-off. At 80% resellable, one fifth of everything that comes back charges you the landed product cost twice: once when you bought it, once when you cannot sell it at the price you planned. Most margin calculators fold that into an average and lose sight of it entirely. This one gives it its own dial so you can watch what happens to the margin row when you drag it from 80% down to 50%.

What the two margin ratios actually mean

This page prints contribution margin as a share of two different bases, and the difference between them is a consolidation worth understanding rather than skipping. CM as a percent of net revenue divides the returns-adjusted margin by the revenue you actually keep after refunds, the number that lands on your P&L. CM as a percent of gross price divides the same margin by the sticker price, which is the base every non-returns-adjusted calculator uses. The gross-price version is lower for the same order, because the denominator includes revenue that never arrives. Use the net figure when you are comparing this unit against your own financials. Use the gross figure when you are comparing it against a margin benchmark, like the ones quoted above, that was never built with returns in mind.

Break-even units move by more than the return rate would suggest

Fixed costs get repaid by contribution margin, one unit at a time, and a lower per-unit margin means more units are required to clear the same fixed cost. That relationship is not linear with the return rate, because returns lower the margin from two directions at once, the refunded revenue and the unrecovered product cost, while the handling fee adds a third small drag on top. The extra units line on this page is the sentence that sells the tool: it is the number of additional units, past what gross margin would have required, that returns force you to sell before your fixed costs are covered. On the default apparel numbers that gap is not small, and it tends to surprise operators who have only ever budgeted off the gross margin row.

Once you have both numbers side by side, three levers move the gap between them. The return rate, which sizing guidance, fit content and product photography all touch. The resellable share, which is a grading and liquidation question more than a marketing one. And the fixed cost base itself, which decides how much that per-unit gap costs you every month it goes unaddressed. Run the same unit at a 22% return rate and again at 18%, and watch what happens to the extra units line before you decide which lever to pull first.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this math

Why is contribution margin lower than gross margin once returns are added?
Gross margin assumes every unit shipped stays sold. Contribution margin multiplies revenue by the kept share, nets product cost against the resellable share, and adds a handling cost for the units expected back. Shipping and the other variable costs are the same in both rows, so the whole gap between gross margin and contribution margin is the price of returns, shown as a dollar figure rather than left as a sentence.
What does resellable at full price mean, and how do I estimate it?
It is the share of returned units that can go straight back into A-grade stock and sell at the original price. Count a month of returns and sort them: back on the shelf at full price, sold at markdown, moved to outlet, written off. The first bucket divided by the total is your resellable share. Most apparel operators who count it for the first time find it lower than they assumed, especially on items that ship in peak season and come back after it.
Why do outbound shipping and other variable costs stay in the math for returned units?
The carrier and the processor have already been paid. The parcel went out, the label was bought, the fee cleared. Reducing those lines in proportion to the return rate would flatter the answer by pretending the cost can be un-spent. Return handling is counted separately, on the expected share of units that come back, so the two costs never double up.
What is the difference between CM as a percent of net revenue and CM as a percent of gross price?
Net revenue is what you actually keep after refunds, so CM as a percent of net revenue is the number that matches your P&L. Gross price is the sticker price before any refunds happen, which is the base almost every non-returns-adjusted margin benchmark uses. The gross-price version reads lower for the same unit, because its denominator includes revenue that never arrives. Compare against your own books using the net figure and against outside benchmarks using the gross one.
Why do returns increase my break-even units by more than the return rate alone would suggest?
Returns lower contribution margin from three directions at once: refunded revenue, unrecovered product cost on the non-resellable share, and the handling fee. Since break-even units is fixed costs divided by contribution margin, a lower margin per unit means a disproportionately larger number of units is needed to clear the same fixed cost base. The extra units line on this page is that gap, computed directly rather than approximated.
What return rate should I enter if I sell apparel?
Pull your own, over a window long enough for returns to land. Returns lag the sale by weeks, so a trailing 30 day rate during a growth month reads low. The published figures quoted on this page cover all retail, all online retail and an apparel-specific range, and they are there to sanity-check the number you enter rather than to replace it. If you have no reliable figure yet, run the calculator twice, a few points apart, and see whether the verdict changes.
