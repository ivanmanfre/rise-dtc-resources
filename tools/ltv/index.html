How to read it

Why lifetime value needs a returns adjustment before it means anything

Lifetime value built on gross AOV treats every order as if it landed and stayed landed. In apparel, a meaningful share of orders come back, and what happens next matters more than the return rate on its own. A shopper who orders a medium and a large to keep one has not walked away from the brand, and swapping for the right size is not the same event as asking for a refund. A lifetime value model that cannot tell those two apart either overstates every customer by ignoring returns entirely, or understates every customer by treating a size swap as lost revenue. This calculator asks for one extra number, the share of returns that come back as exchanges rather than refunds, and lets that single input carry the difference.

The two ways a return shows up in the model

Net AOV is gross AOV multiplied by one minus the return rate times one minus the exchange share. Only the true refund share removes revenue. An exchange keeps the dollars in the business, even though the size or the color changed. That is the whole adjustment: a return rate of 40% that is three quarters exchanges costs the business a tenth of revenue, not four tenths, because most of what came back went straight back out the door in a different size. This page does not model the receiving, inspection and repack cost of that swap, since it is a lifetime revenue view, not a unit cost view. The true profit per order tool carries that cost line if you need it alongside this one.

What purchase frequency times lifespan actually models

Total orders across the relationship is purchase frequency multiplied by lifespan in years, an average across the whole customer base rather than a curve fitted to any one shopper. It treats every year of the relationship as buying at the same rate, which is a simplification in the same family as a geometric repeat rate approximation. Nobody actually orders in neat, even intervals, but averaged across enough customers the two numbers you enter, how often and for how long, describe the shape of the relationship well enough to price it. Contribution margin then applies to net AOV on every one of those orders, so the returns adjustment compounds correctly across the full lifespan instead of being subtracted once at the end as an afterthought.

Reading contribution LTV against the naive number

Naive contribution LTV is the same formula run on gross AOV instead of net AOV, the number you would get if you forgot returns existed. The gap between the two is what returns take out of a customer's lifetime value, and it is worth watching on its own, separate from the headline. A brand with a high return rate but a high exchange share can still show a small gap, because most of what looks like a return risk is actually a shopper finding the right size and staying. A brand with a low return rate but a low exchange share can show a bigger gap than expected, because every one of those returns is a full refund rather than a swap.

The LTV to CAC ratio, and where the 3 to 1 rule comes from

When a blended CAC is entered, the page divides contribution LTV by CAC to produce a ratio, and compares it against David Skok's widely repeated rule, quoted verbatim with its link in the panel above, that a ratio above 3 is healthy. That rule was written about SaaS businesses, where a customer pays a predictable amount every month and the cost structure looks nothing like a physical apparel order with a return rate and a resellable share. It is printed on this page with its origin named rather than treated as a fixed pass mark, because a DTC brand with thin margins and a real returns problem may need a wider ratio to carry the same risk a SaaS company carries at 3 to 1. Use it as a reference point, not a verdict. If the CAC field is left blank, the ratio and the net LTV both disappear rather than showing a number built on a guess.

Why the range flexes purchase frequency

Purchase frequency is the number most founders round to a guess from memory. Lifespan is usually read off a longer, steadier cohort curve, so it carries less uncertainty per unit of effort to check it. Flexing both inputs at once would compound two different kinds of guesswork into a spread wide enough to be useless. This page holds lifespan fixed and flexes frequency by 20% in each direction instead, and if you want to see frequency and lifespan pushed against each other at once, the LTV to CAC and payback tool runs that wider two input view.

Discounting is optional, and most apparel brands can leave it off

The discount toggle converts future years of contribution into today's dollars at the annual rate you set, using end of year timing with the trailing partial year prorated into the next period. At a lifespan of one or two years, the difference between the discounted and undiscounted figure is usually small enough not to change a decision, which is why the toggle defaults to off. It starts to matter more once lifespan stretches past three or four years or the discount rate climbs, situations closer to a long term subscription model than a typical apparel repeat customer.

Once you have the number, three dials move it the most. Exchange share, which is a sizing and fit content question as much as a returns policy question. Purchase frequency, which email, SMS and replenishment timing all touch directly. And blended CAC, read off the bank statement rather than the number the ad platform reports, since the platform only counts the orders it believes it caused.

Questions we get

Five things founders ask about this math

Why do exchanges count differently than a refund in this calculator?
A refund removes the revenue from the order. An exchange keeps it, because the same dollars stay in the business even though the size or color changed. This calculator asks for the share of returns that come back as exchanges rather than assuming every return is a refund, which is the assumption that quietly overstates lifetime value the most in apparel.
What return rate and exchange share should I enter for an apparel brand?
Pull your own numbers over a window long enough for returns to land, since returns lag the sale by weeks. Count a month of returns and split them into two piles: the ones that came back as a refund and the ones that came back as a request for a different size or color. The second pile divided by the total is your exchange share.
Is the 3 to 1 LTV to CAC ratio the right bar for a DTC apparel brand?
The 3 to 1 figure comes from David Skok's SaaS Metrics work at Matrix Partners, quoted with its link in the published figures panel on this page, and it travels with subscription assumptions that do not map cleanly onto apparel. This page prints the ratio and names where the benchmark comes from so you can weigh it against your own margin structure rather than treat it as a fixed pass mark.
Why does the range only flex purchase frequency and hold lifespan fixed?
Purchase frequency is the number most founders round to a guess, while lifespan usually comes from a longer, steadier retention curve. Flexing both at once would compound two guesses into a spread wide enough to be useless. For the wider two input view, the LTV to CAC and payback tool runs both dials against each other.
What if I do not know my blended CAC yet?
Leave the field blank. The lifetime value still computes in full, it is only the net figure and the ratio against acquisition cost that disappear until you enter one. A rough CAC from your bank statement, spend divided by new customers, is enough to start.
