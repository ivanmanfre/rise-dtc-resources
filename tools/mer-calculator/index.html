How to read it

What one blended ratio leaves out

Marketing efficiency ratio is total revenue divided by total ad spend, and on its own it never touches a return. An order that ships and an order that ships and comes back both count the same in the numerator the day the sale posts. That is fine for a rough read of the whole account, and it is exactly why a single blended MER on a dashboard can sit at a comfortable-looking number while the business underneath it is thin. Apparel carries this gap further than most categories, because sizing gets decided at home and a share of every order comes back after the ad that bought it has already cleared.

Break-even MER is a different question: not what ratio are you running, but what ratio does your margin actually require. It is built from the contribution rate after returns, which folds in four things at once. A kept order delivers your full contribution margin. A returned order delivers none of it, because the sale reverses. A returned order also costs you a share of AOV to process, the return shipping, the inspection, and the markdown on the units that cannot go back on the shelf at full price. And the whole calculation runs against gross revenue booked, the number your ad platform and your bank statement both agree on before any of that unwinds. Multiply that contribution rate through and invert it, and the result is the minimum blended MER before the ad account stops paying for itself.

Why the floor moves when the return rate does

Return rate sits inside the contribution rate twice: once directly, as the share of orders that contribute nothing, and once through the return cost dial, since a higher return rate means more orders carrying that processing cost. Push the return rate up and the contribution rate after returns falls, which pushes the required MER up in response. That is the whole reason this page asks for a return rate at all when the plain MER formula does not use one. The break-even range further up the page recomputes that floor five points higher and five points lower than what you entered, independently at each end, because return rate is usually the input a founder is estimating rather than reading off a report.

How blended MER and aMER differ

Blended MER divides the same ad spend by every dollar of revenue, including repeat orders a returning customer placed without a fresh ad touching them. aMER divides that same spend by revenue from first-time customers only. When aMER sits meaningfully below blended MER, a real share of the ratio is being carried by customers who were already acquired, and the number on the dashboard is flattering the current spend more than the current spend is actually earning. Leaving the new customer revenue field off keeps aMER out of the page entirely instead of quietly reading as zero, which would understate it in the other direction.

The floor changes again once fixed costs join it

Break-even MER, as computed above, is an ad-only number: spend against contribution, nothing else in it. Most accounts also carry rent, salaries and software that ad spend alone was never meant to cover. The all-in floor on the math card adds that fixed operating cost on top of ad spend before dividing by the same contribution rate, so it sits at or above the ad-only floor whenever a fixed cost is entered, and matches it exactly when the fixed cost field is left at zero.

Reading the verdict

The tier under the headline number reads the gap between your blended MER and the break-even MER it is measured against. Comfortably above the floor means the account is carrying real cushion above what the margin requires. Above the floor with no cushion means the account clears today but has no room if the return rate or the margin moves against it. Below the floor means the ad spend, at the contribution rate you entered, is not paying for itself yet, whatever the raw MER number looks like on a platform dashboard. When the contribution rate after returns falls to zero or below, no MER breaks even, and the page says so directly instead of printing a floor that implies more ad spend can fix a margin problem.

Once you have the number, three dials move it. The return rate, which sizing guidance and fit content both touch before a marketing dial ever does. The contribution margin behind the price, which sits upstream of every ad decision. And the ad spend itself, which is the only lever that changes the ratio without changing the floor underneath it.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this math

What counts as ad spend in this calculator?
Every platform combined for the period, Meta, Google, TikTok, affiliates, all of it. Blended ad spend is what your bank account actually paid across all of them.
Why does return rate change the MER I need, when MER itself never touches returns?
MER is just revenue divided by spend, so it never sees a return. Break-even MER does, because it is built from your contribution margin after returns come out. A returned order keeps none of the margin and costs a share of AOV to process, so the more orders that come back, the more revenue you need per dollar of ad spend before the account clears.
What is aMER and why does it usually sit below blended MER?
aMER divides the same ad spend by revenue from first-time customers only. Blended MER counts every order, including the repeat ones a returning customer places without a fresh ad touching them. Once aMER runs meaningfully below blended MER, most of the ratio is being carried by customers you already acquired.
My return rate and contribution margin are both estimates. How far off could my floor be?
The break-even range on this page recomputes the floor with your return rate five points higher and five points lower, independently at each end. If the whole range sits below where you are running, the estimate is not the risk. If your number sits inside the range, pull the real return rate before you change ad spend on it.
What happens if my contribution rate after returns is zero or negative?
No MER breaks even. At that contribution rate every additional dollar of revenue returns nothing or less than nothing after returns are handled, so no amount of MER, however high, pays for the ad spend. The fix is the contribution rate itself: the return rate, the return cost, or the margin behind the price.
Does the break-even MER include my fixed operating costs?
Only the ad-only floor sits in the headline number. Break-even MER is spend against contribution, nothing else in it. The all-in floor further down the math card adds your fixed operating cost on top, so leave that field at zero for the ad-only number and fill it in for the floor that matches your full P&L.
