How to read it

Why a repeat rate and an exchange rate get counted as the same thing

A repeat purchase rate answers one question: did the same customer come back and buy again. In apparel, that question has a quiet complication that most dashboards never surface. When a customer orders a medium, decides it runs small, and asks for a large instead, most storefronts and helpdesk tools process that as a brand new order. The customer never chose to buy from you a second time. They chose a different size for the same purchase. Your reporting has no way to tell the difference, so it counts a size swap the same way it counts a customer who came back a season later for a different item entirely.

That miscount compounds because exchanges are common in apparel and rare almost everywhere else. A category with a wide spread in fit, cut and sizing across brands generates more of these replacement orders than a category where sizing is fixed, like electronics or supplements. So the categories most likely to lean on repeat rate as a growth story are the same categories where the raw number is most likely to be inflated by something that has nothing to do with loyalty.

What the exchange-adjusted rate actually removes

This calculator asks for one number most repeat-rate tools never ask for: the share of second orders, inside your repeat count, that were created by an exchange rather than a genuine return purchase. Multiply your repeat customer count by one minus that share, and what is left is an estimate of customers who repeated because they wanted more, not because a garment failed to fit. The gap between the raw rate and this true rate is printed on the page as exchange inflation, in points, so the miscount has a number attached to it instead of living as a caveat in a footnote.

The math treats this as an expected value rather than a headcount. So a 20% exchange share does not mean exactly one in five specific customers is an exchange case. It means that, across the window, roughly a fifth of what your dashboard calls a repeat customer is more accurately described as the same original order, resolved in a second shipment. Expected values can be fractional, which is why this page prints customer counts to one decimal rather than rounding them into people who do not exist.

Why the revenue impact runs on net AOV

The lift model on this page answers a forward-looking question: if you raised the true repeat rate by a target number of points, what would that be worth. It multiplies the newly repeating customers by one additional order each, at your average order value net of refunds, then applies your contribution margin. Net AOV matters here for the same reason returns matter across every other page in this set: a dollar of gross revenue that comes back as a refund was never really a dollar you kept. Using net AOV keeps the lift estimate from repeating the same optimistic mistake the raw repeat rate makes, just measured in currency instead of customers.

The lift model also makes one assumption explicit rather than folding it into the formula: one additional order per newly repeating customer. It is not a lifetime value calculation and does not compound across future orders. That is a deliberate, conservative choice. The compounding version of this question, where a customer keeps repeating across a longer horizon, lives on the lifetime value calculator. This page answers a narrower question: what is the next order worth, one lift target at a time.

Why the window matters more than it looks like it should

A repeat rate without a stated window is close to meaningless, because the rate mechanically climbs the longer you measure. A 3 month window will always show fewer repeat customers than a 12 month window pulled from the same store, not because loyalty changed but because customers had less time to place a second order. This page asks for the window directly and uses it to annualize the contribution impact, so a 6 month measurement and an 18 month measurement can both be compared against the same yearly number instead of two figures that were never on the same footing.

What this page does not do

There is no published, source-checked benchmark for apparel repeat purchase rate, for the share of apparel returns processed as exchanges, or for the cohort window most reporting uses, that met the sourcing bar for this page. Repeat rate reporting varies so widely by return policy, sizing system and platform default that a single quoted figure would do more harm than good sitting next to your number. So this page prints none. Every figure here comes from what you enter, with the arithmetic shown underneath it.

The number worth watching

Exchange inflation points is the number that tells you whether your reported repeat rate can be trusted for a scaling decision. When it sits well below your raw rate, the exchange caveat is a rounding issue and the true rate is close enough to the reported one that either number will do. When it climbs toward half the raw rate or past it, as it does for stores with a wide-fit product line, the reported number is telling a story that is mostly about sizing rather than loyalty. In that case the fix is a returns and exchange reporting one: get exchange orders tagged separately in your platform so this number stops needing to be estimated at all.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this math

Why do exchanges inflate my repeat purchase rate?
Most storefronts and helpdesk platforms create a brand new order when a customer exchanges a size, so a size swap gets recorded exactly like a customer who came back on their own. Multiplying your repeat count by one minus the exchange share removes those replacement orders from the count of customers who chose to repeat.
How do I find my exchange share of repeats?
Pull a report from your returns or exchange platform filtered to the reason code for exchanges, then divide that count by your total repeat orders in the same window. If your platform does not tag exchanges separately, sample a month of second orders and check for the same product in a different size shipped within a few days of the first order, which is the pattern an exchange leaves behind.
What happens to the true rate range when my exchange share is zero?
The range collapses to a single number, since there is no guess left to flex, and the page states that instead of drawing a spread around a number with nothing to estimate.
Why does the lift impact use net AOV instead of gross AOV?
Net AOV is what you keep once refunds are removed, and the whole point of the exchange adjustment is to stop counting activity that was never new demand. Running the lift impact on gross AOV would undo that work by pricing the extra orders at a number that includes revenue you never actually kept.
What does the lift model assume, and what does it not do?
It assumes one additional order per newly repeating customer, at your net AOV and contribution margin, stated on the page rather than hidden in the formula. It is not a lifetime value calculation and does not compound across future orders. For the compounding version of this question, use the lifetime value calculator instead.
What if the customers with 2 or more orders I enter is higher than my total customers?
That is a data entry mismatch rather than a real repeat rate, since a repeat customer count can never exceed the total customer count it was drawn from. Recheck that both numbers come from the same window and the same customer definition before trusting anything else on this page.
