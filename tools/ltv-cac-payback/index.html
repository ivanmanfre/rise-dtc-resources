How to read it

Why the ratio comes back as a range

Two numbers get repeated on every ecommerce panel and podcast until they sound like facts: the LTV to CAC ratio quoted in the panel above, and a clean number of months to pay it back. Both came from somewhere real. Both get quoted with a precision the underlying inputs cannot support. This calculator prints the same ratio and the same payback window, but it prints them as a range, because two of the seven numbers that feed the math are estimates rather than receipts.

Apparel is the hard case, and it is the case these defaults are built around. A shopper orders two sizes to keep one, and that single order carries a return that a plain revenue dashboard never sees. The order value you actually keep is what is left after that return rate, with one adjustment most spreadsheets skip: an exchange is not a refund. When a returned item comes back as a different size instead of money, the revenue stays in the business. This page asks for that split explicitly, as exchange share of returns, so the net order value reflects what apparel returns actually do rather than treating every return as a lost sale.

Net order value is where the whole ratio starts

Net order value multiplies the gross order by one minus the return rate times one minus the exchange share. At the apparel defaults, a 22% return rate with 30% of those returns becoming exchanges nets out to a smaller haircut than 22% alone would suggest, because roughly a third of what looks like lost revenue is actually a customer keeping a different size. Contribution margin then converts that net order value into the dollars one order actually leaves before acquisition spend. Multiply by purchase frequency and by the years a customer keeps buying, and the result is lifetime value: the total contribution one customer is worth across the whole relationship, beyond the first sale.

The ratio and the payback period answer different questions

The ratio answers whether the relationship is worth having at all. Divide lifetime value by blended CAC, and a number above 1 means the customer eventually pays for themselves. A number above 3, the threshold David Skok's SaaS Metrics work put on the map, is where SaaS operators historically called a business healthy enough to keep funding. The payback period answers a different, more urgent question: how long does the cash stay underwater before the acquisition spend comes back. A brand can carry an excellent ratio and a payback period that quietly outlives the customer, because the ratio is a lifetime total and payback is a monthly clock. This page runs both, because a founder who only checks the ratio can miss a cash problem that shows up first in the payback line.

Payback here compares monthly contribution, contribution per order times purchase frequency divided by twelve, against the CAC spent to acquire the customer. It is a smooth average, not the real, lumpy timing of actual reorders, and it is meant to be read that way, as a planning number rather than a receipt. The orders-to-repay line is its discrete companion, the number of orders, rounded up, that this specific customer needs to place before the acquisition cost clears. When contribution per order is healthy, that number is often two or three. When it is thin, it can climb past what the customer's own purchase frequency delivers inside their lifespan, and when that happens, the page says so directly rather than printing a large month count that implies patience will eventually fix it.

Why Skok's 3 to 1 rule needs a caveat on a returns-heavy brand

The 3 to 1 target comes from David Skok's SaaS Metrics work at Matrix Partners, quoted verbatim with its link in the panel above, and it describes SaaS companies, where a customer either keeps a subscription or cancels it and there is no returns line at all. A DTC apparel brand carries a returns and exchange dynamic that a SaaS business never has to model, which is exactly why net order value sits at the top of this calculator's math instead of being assumed away. Two brands can enter the same gross AOV and blended CAC and land on very different ratios once return rate and exchange share are accounted for honestly. Treat the 3 to 1 figure as a sanity check on the shape of the answer, not as a target this specific math was built to hit.

Why frequency and margin move together in the spread

Purchase frequency and contribution margin are flexed jointly in the pessimistic and optimistic cases, both down 20% together or both up 20% together, because they tend to move together in practice. A brand that is winning on margin is usually also winning on repeat frequency, from the same causes: better product fit, fewer sizing mistakes, a customer who trusts the brand enough to come back. Flexing them independently would generate scenarios that rarely happen and would make the spread wider than the real uncertainty in the business. Lifespan is left alone because it is usually set by category convention rather than re-estimated every time, and CAC is left alone because it is the one number in this whole page that comes straight off a bank statement instead of a guess.

Read the spread the way you would read any honest estimate: if the whole band clears the ratio you actually need, and the whole band pays back inside the customer's lifespan, the estimate has stopped being the risk and the underlying economics have taken over. If the band straddles the line either way, that is the signal to go get a truer number for purchase frequency and margin before committing more acquisition spend to this order.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this math

Why is the ratio shown as a range instead of one number?
Because purchase frequency and contribution margin, two of the seven inputs, are usually estimates rather than measured facts. Printing a single ratio to two decimals makes those estimates look like receipts. This calculator flexes both inputs 20% in each direction and shows the resulting band first, with the point estimate stated second and named for what it is, a midpoint rather than a guarantee.
Where does the 3 to 1 LTV to CAC target come from, and does it apply to apparel and DTC brands?
It comes from David Skok's SaaS Metrics work at Matrix Partners, quoted verbatim with its link in the published figures panel on this page, which describes SaaS companies with a ratio above 3, and sometimes as high as 7 or 8, as healthy enough to keep funding. That figure was built for subscription software with no returns line at all. A DTC apparel brand carries return rate and exchange share directly into net order value before the ratio math even starts, so treat 3 to 1 as a sanity check on the shape of the answer rather than a target this calculator was built to hit.
What is the difference between the ratio and the payback period?
The ratio answers whether the relationship is worth having at all, across the customer's entire lifespan. The payback period answers how long the acquisition cost stays underwater before the cash comes back, on a monthly clock. A brand can show a strong ratio and still carry a payback period long enough to strain cash flow, because the ratio is a lifetime total and payback is a running monthly balance.
Why are purchase frequency and margin flexed together instead of separately?
They tend to move together in real businesses. A brand executing well on margin, through better product fit and fewer sizing mistakes, usually earns more repeat purchases from the same causes. Flexing them jointly produces a spread that matches how these two numbers actually move, rather than manufacturing scenarios where one improves while the other falls, which is rare in practice.
What happens when the payback period is longer than the customer's lifespan?
The page names it directly instead of printing a month count that implies patience will fix it. If the entered purchase frequency and margin never generate enough monthly contribution to clear acquisition cost before the customer's lifespan ends, on average that customer never fully repays what it cost to acquire them, even though the ratio itself might still read above 1.
How does an exchange differ from a return in this math, and why does it matter?
A refund removes the order's revenue from the business. An exchange keeps it, because the customer still owns something they paid for, just in a different size or style. This calculator asks for the share of returns that become exchanges specifically so that net order value, and everything computed from it, reflects the revenue you actually keep rather than treating every return as a full loss.
