How to read it

Why the honest number is almost always lower

The ratio the category quotes almost always divides gross email revenue by what the platform and your time cost, full stop. Nothing about the subscribers on the list and nothing about the orders that come back ever enters that denominator or that numerator. It is a real number, and it explains why email keeps getting called the highest-ROI channel available. It is also not the number that tells you whether the channel is actually growing the business, because a list built for free will always beat a list you paid to fill, and a store that never adjusts for returns will always look healthier than one that does.

Apparel is the case where the gap shows up fastest. A flow that reminds a shopper about a size they left in the cart earns credit for a sale that a size chart and a fit photo will decide anyway, and a chunk of that sale comes back a week later because the size guessed wrong. The flow gets full credit for the order and none of the blame for the return. This calculator prices the return back in with one number, the net cost of a returned order, applied as a share of order value across the whole return rate. It is not a unit-by-unit teardown the way an order-level tool would run it. It is a channel-level proxy built to answer one question fast: does the email program, taken as a whole, actually pay for itself once the returns it also generates are accounted for.

What the two revenue lines are for

Flow revenue and campaign revenue are kept apart on purpose. Flows run on autopilot once they are built: welcome, abandoned cart, post-purchase, winback. Campaigns take a person's time every single send. A list that earns most of its revenue from flows is earning it with a fixed cost that does not scale with headcount. A list that earns most of its revenue from campaigns is buying that revenue with agency or in-house hours every week. Neither split is automatically better, but conflating them into one "email revenue" line hides which of the two costs is actually driving the return, and that is the cost most stores actually have room to change.

What gets consolidated, and why

Platform cost and agency or in-house time are added together into one run cost, because both are paid whether or not a single new subscriber joins the list this period. Subscriber acquisition cost is kept separate, because it only exists if you are actively buying the list rather than growing it for free through a popup nobody paid to place traffic against. The honest ROI divides returns-adjusted contribution by the sum of all three: run cost plus acquisition cost. That is the whole consolidation. Nothing about the return rate, the flow and campaign split, or the margin gets folded into a single blended figure anywhere upstream of that division, so every input on this page is still visible in the rows above.

The dial almost nobody fills in

Subscriber acquisition cost is the input most stores leave at zero, not because it is genuinely zero but because nobody has gone and added up what the popup traffic, the giveaway spend and the lead ads actually cost per new name. Leave it blank and the ROI on this page looks stable and high, because the list is quietly being treated as free. The moment a store starts paying for reach through paid social, cold traffic to a landing page, or a giveaway with a media buy behind it, that cost belongs in this field, and the honest figure will move, sometimes by a lot, the first time it is filled in correctly.

Breakeven subscriber acquisition cost is the number to watch

Breakeven subscriber acquisition cost answers a narrower question than the ROI does: given what this period actually earned after run cost, what is the most you could pay per new subscriber before email stops paying for itself. When that number comes back negative, the program does not even cover platform and time before a single subscriber is bought, and no acquisition budget fixes that. When it comes back comfortably above what you are actually paying, there is real room to spend more on growing the list without breaking the channel's own economics.

Three mistakes show up most often once a store starts running this math for real. The first is reading the headline ratio off a marketer survey and assuming it describes their own list, when it describes a self-reported average across a very wide range of stores and spend levels. The second is leaving new subscribers and subscriber acquisition cost both at zero long after the store started paying for growth, which is the single biggest reason a program looks safer than it is. The third is pricing returns off a memory of last year's rate rather than a number pulled this period, on a channel where the return rate moves with whatever the flows and campaigns happened to sell that month. Fix those three and the number on this page starts matching what the bank account actually shows.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this math

Why does this page print two ROI numbers instead of one?
The number the category quotes usually divides gross email revenue by what the platform and your time cost, and nothing else. This page prints that ratio too, labeled as the headline figure, then prints a second one that also subtracts what returns take back and what you paid to get the subscribers on the list. The gap between the two is the part most reports leave out.
Why do returns lower the ROI on email at all? The subscriber already bought once.
A subscriber who buys and then returns the order still cost you the send, the platform fee and, on paid growth, the acquisition spend, and the sale itself hands back most of its contribution. This calculator prices that in with one rate: the share of orders returned and a net cost of a returned order, applied to the whole return share rather than tracked unit by unit. It is a channel-level proxy built for speed, not a full order teardown.
What should I put in agency or in-house time cost?
Whatever it actually costs to plan, write, design and send the flows and campaigns this period, whether that is an invoice, a contractor rate or an internal hourly estimate for the hours spent. It sits next to platform cost as the running cost of the channel, before a single subscriber is counted.
Is it fine to use last-click attribution for flow and campaign revenue?
It is what most stores have on hand, and this calculator takes it as entered. Last-click email attribution typically overstates what flows would have earned anyway, since it credits email for orders a shopper was already going to place. Treat the honest ROI figure as a ceiling on the channel rather than an exact number, and it will not mislead you.
What happens if I have not tracked what a new subscriber costs?
Leave new subscribers and subscriber acquisition cost at zero and the ROI still computes, using only platform and time cost as the denominator. The page flags that the result looks stable only because the list is being treated as free. The moment you start paying for popups, giveaways or lead ads, fill both fields in and watch the honest figure move.
Why is Litmus's $36 or 45 to 1 figure so much higher than what I get here?
Both come from Litmus's 2025 State of Email survey of about 500 marketers, quoted with its link in the published figures panel on this page, and they describe email against platform and time cost only, the same headline ratio this page prints as the naive figure. Once subscriber acquisition cost and the returns rate are added to the denominator and the numerator respectively, the honest figure on this page is almost always lower, and on a young or paid list it can be a fraction of the naive one.
