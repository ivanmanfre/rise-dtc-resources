How to read it

Why cost per order only means something next to a ceiling

Cost per acquisition, priced on its own, is a fact with no opinion attached. Ad spend divided by orders tells you what you paid. It does not tell you whether that was a good price, and a number without a ceiling next to it gets treated as good or bad based on how it feels rather than what the order can actually carry. This calculator prices both halves: what you are paying, and the most that order can afford to cost, before you ever look at whether the number feels high.

CPA and CAC answer different questions, and apparel brands run into the gap between them constantly. CPA prices one order. CAC prices one customer. The two match exactly when every customer places a single order and never returns, which almost never happens once a repeat purchase program is working. A brand that prices only CPA and ignores the customer who comes back three more times inside the year is pricing the front door and ignoring the house behind it. This calculator keeps both in view: an order-one ceiling that never assumes a repeat purchase, and a second ceiling that counts the repeat orders you enter, inside a window you choose.

The order-one ceiling is a returns-adjusted contribution rate

The order-one ceiling folds three things into a single number per order: the revenue you keep after the return rate, the contribution margin on that revenue, and what a returned order costs you net of whatever you can resell. A brand can hit the same margin two different ways, a low return rate with an expensive return process, or a high return rate with a cheap one, and this calculator asks for both the rate and the cost precisely so those two paths do not collapse into one number that hides which lever actually moves the ceiling. Apparel sits at the sharp end of this. A returned garment that shipped in a size that no longer sells at full price does not cost the same as a returned garment that goes straight back on the shelf, even at an identical return rate.

Because the order-one ceiling rests entirely on inputs you can look up today, your margin, your return rate, your AOV, it is published as a single number rather than a range. There is nothing to forecast in it.

The window ceiling adds a forecast, and prices it honestly

The second ceiling adds the repeat orders you expect a customer to place beyond the first, inside a payback window you choose. That repeat count is closer to a forecast than a fact for most brands, which is why this calculator publishes it as a range rather than a single figure: the return rate flexed 5 points in either direction, the repeat count flexed 25% in either direction. A brand willing to wait twelve months to get its money back can afford to pay more per order than a brand that needs the spend back in three, because a longer window credits more of the repeat purchases a customer is expected to make. Pick the window you can genuinely wait out. A ceiling built on patience you do not have will not hold up when you spend against it.

Repeat orders are assumed to land evenly across the twelve months, so a six month window credits half of what you entered and a zero month window credits none of it. That is a modeling choice, stated plainly rather than hidden inside the arithmetic. Real repeat purchase timing clusters around restock cycles, gifting seasons and replenishment intervals that vary by category, so treat the window ceiling as a range to reason with, not a date to promise a finance partner.

Reading the verdict

When your actual cost per order sits at or under the order-one ceiling, the spend pays for itself before a single repeat order ever lands, the cleanest position on this page. When it sits above that ceiling but under the window ceiling, the spend pays back, but only once the repeat orders inside your chosen window arrive, which means you are funding the gap between the two numbers out of cash on hand until they do. When it sits above even the window ceiling, the spend does not pay back on the assumptions entered, and the honest next step is to move whichever input you can actually move: the spend itself, the margin, the return rate, or how long you are willing to wait.

Three things move the ceiling more than founders expect. The return rate, because it discounts both the order-one ceiling and everything repeat orders add on top of it. The return cost, because two brands at the identical return rate can carry very different ceilings depending on what a return actually costs to process. And the payback window, because it is the one input that turns a forecast about future customers into a number you can compare against today's spend.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this math

What is the difference between CPA here and CAC on the other calculator?
CPA is the cost of one order. CAC is the cost of one customer. They match exactly when every customer places one order and never again, and they pull apart the moment repeat orders enter the picture. This calculator prices the order itself, first alone and then inside a repeat window you choose, which is why it sits next to the CAC calculator rather than standing in for it.
Why does the ceiling change when I move the payback window?
The window sets how many of the repeat orders you entered count toward the ceiling. Repeat orders are assumed to land evenly across twelve months, so a six month window counts half of the repeat orders you entered, and a zero month window counts none of them, which collapses the ceiling back to the order-one number. Pick the window you can actually wait, not the longest one available.
Why are there two ceilings instead of one number?
The order-one ceiling rests on inputs you can look up today: your margin, your return rate, your average order value. The window ceiling adds a repeat order count, which is closer to a forecast than a fact for most brands. Publishing both keeps the number you can defend on its own next to the number that depends on customers you have not acquired yet.
What return rate and repeat order count should apparel brands use?
Pull your own numbers rather than a published average. Size runs, seasonal timing and the fit of a specific style move both figures more than a category benchmark can capture. The Google Search figures printed further down this page describe overall categories, not your store, and they are there to sanity check the numbers you enter, not replace them.
Why does the calculator ask for a return cost on top of a return rate?
The return rate tells you how many orders come back. The return cost tells you what each one costs once it does: the product you do not fully recover, the label, the handling. A brand with a low return rate but an expensive return process can end up with the same ceiling as a brand with the opposite mix, and folding both into one number would hide which lever actually moves it.
If my spend is above the ceiling, does that always mean stop?
It means the spend does not pay for itself on the assumptions entered above. Sometimes the fix is the spend. Sometimes it is the margin, the return rate or how long you are willing to wait for repeat orders to land. The verdict tells you which side of the ceiling you are on. What you do about it depends on which input you can actually move.
