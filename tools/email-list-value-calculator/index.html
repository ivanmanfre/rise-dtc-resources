How to read it

What the average subscriber value hides

The oldest question in email marketing is what a subscriber is worth, and the oldest wrong answer is to divide ninety days of email revenue by however many names sit on the list. That division always produces a real number, and it is almost always the wrong one, because a list built up over two or three years carries thousands of addresses that have not opened anything in months. Averaging revenue across a list like that credits the silent majority with the same value as the customer opening every send, and the flat number that comes out the other end understates what the engaged core is worth while overstating what the list as a whole is worth to anyone deciding whether to keep growing it.

Apparel lists make the problem easy to see, because apparel brands acquire subscribers fast and lose their attention just as fast. A discount popup during a seasonal spike pulls names in quickly, and a large share of them came for the discount, bought once if at all, and never opened another email. Run that pattern through enough seasons and the list keeps growing while the flat revenue-per-subscriber number falls, which reads on a dashboard as the list getting worse, when what actually happened is the active core stayed roughly the same size while a large inactive tail got bolted on beside it.

Why active share is the input this page adds

Every input on this page except one shows up on some version of a list ROI tool. Active share does not, and it is the one that moves the answer the most. This calculator asks what portion of the list opened or clicked in the last ninety days, splits the list into active and inactive on that basis, and reports two per-subscriber numbers side by side: the naive rate, gross revenue divided by the whole list, and the honest rate, the same revenue divided only by the subscribers who are still there. On a list with a thirty percent active share, the honest number runs more than three times the naive one, because the entire ninety days of revenue is being credited to less than a third of the names on the file. Neither number is fake. They answer two different questions, and only the active rate is useful for pricing a re-engagement campaign or a list-growth spend.

Flow and campaign revenue stay separate, then get consolidated on purpose

The ninety-day revenue figure on this page consolidates flow revenue, the welcome series, abandoned cart, post-purchase and the rest of the automated sequences, with campaign revenue, the one-off sends scheduled by hand. They are entered separately because they behave differently. Flow revenue keeps arriving on autopilot whether or not a campaign went out that week. Campaign revenue depends entirely on what got sent and when. The page reports the split as a share of the ninety-day total specifically so a consolidated dollar figure never hides which engine is actually doing the work. The example loaded into the fields above splits $42,000 of flow revenue against $28,000 of campaign revenue, so flow carries 60% of the ninety-day total. A list weighted that way is durable in a way a list living on weekly discount blasts is not, and the two brands can carry the exact same total revenue number while sitting on very different footing.

Where returns sit inside the contribution number

List value on this page is reported as returns-adjusted contribution rather than gross revenue, for the same reason every other calculator on this site treats returns as part of the arithmetic rather than a footnote. A kept order returns its full contribution margin. A returned order returns none of it and still costs a share of the order value to process, the label, the inspection, the restock. The contribution rate is built from both facts at once, so the contribution figure is what the list's revenue actually turns into after refunds and handling. The naive per-subscriber figure is printed too, because it is the number every other list value tool reports, and the gap between the two is exactly the point of building this one.

Why the winback number comes back as a range

The points-of-active-share dial is the plan you set, and the page treats it that way. Winning back ten points of active share is priced against the assumption that a reactivated subscriber spends exactly like today's actives do, which is the arithmetic ceiling and almost certainly optimistic. A subscriber who went quiet for ninety days and came back is not the same subscriber as one who never left, so the range on this page runs from half that ceiling to the full ceiling. The honest read leans toward the low end, and the high end is the best case a winback campaign could plausibly hit rather than a number to plan a quarter around.

What this changes about the winback decision

The naive per-subscriber rate makes every list look similar: a bigger list is a bigger number, full stop. The active-share rate makes the real decision visible. A list at thirty percent active with a strong revenue-per-active number is carrying a large inactive tail that a sunset flow or a targeted winback campaign can either reactivate or remove, and either move raises the average without touching a single dollar of revenue. A list already sitting at ninety percent active has almost nothing left to win back, and the honest answer there is that the next dollar of value comes from list growth rather than list health. Both are correct reads of the same ninety-day revenue number, and the flat rate this page replaces cannot tell them apart.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this math

Why does this divide by active subscribers instead of the whole list?
Because inactive subscribers are not producing any of the ninety-day revenue in this model, so crediting them anyway understates what the engaged core is actually worth. This page reports both numbers side by side, the flat rate any list ROI tool reports and the active rate, so you can see how much of the gap between them is dead weight.
What counts as an active subscriber?
Anyone who opened or clicked in the last ninety days. It is the one input a flat list value tool leaves out, and it is the input that decides the whole answer, because it sets how many names the ninety days of revenue actually gets divided across.
Why is the winback value a range instead of one number?
Winning back points of active share is priced against the assumption that a reactivated subscriber spends exactly like today's actives do, which is a ceiling rather than a forecast. Someone who went quiet and came back is not the same as someone who never left, so this page publishes half that ceiling as the low end and the full ceiling as the high end, rather than a single number that would be false precision.
Why are flow and campaign revenue entered separately if they get added together?
Because they behave differently even though the calculator consolidates them into one ninety-day total. Flow revenue keeps arriving on autopilot from the welcome, abandoned cart and post-purchase sequences. Campaign revenue depends entirely on what got sent that week. Reporting the split as a share of the total shows which engine your list actually runs on, without hiding that split inside a single consolidated dollar figure.
Why does the contribution number treat returns as a cost, on top of the lost revenue?
A returned order gives back none of its contribution margin and still costs a share of the order value to process, the label, the inspection, the restock. This page's contribution rate is built from both facts, kept orders paying full margin and returned orders costing you twice, so the contribution figure reflects what the list's revenue actually turns into rather than what it looks like before refunds and handling are taken out.
What happens if my active share is zero or my email revenue is zero?
The per-active numbers and the winback range go blank rather than showing a zero, because dividing revenue across zero active subscribers has no real answer. The naive whole-list rate still prints, since revenue divided by list size is defined even when the active share and revenue are both zero.
