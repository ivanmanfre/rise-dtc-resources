The math

Every line, with your numbers written into it

The unit of analysis is one month of Google spend against one order target. These rows update as you move the dials above.

  • Brand and non-brand are the only two buckets. Performance Max and Shopping fold into non-brand here, because that is where their unbranded traffic and its cost per order belong.
  • Brand clicks are bought before non-brand clicks, because brand costs less per order in every account where this page is worth opening.
  • Brand demand is fixed inside the month and is treated as unaffected by the non-brand spend. Prospecting does create brand searches later, so this understates what brand is worth over time.
  • Returns are counted through contribution, not through the media. Contribution per order is multiplied by the share of orders that stick, and the handling cost of the ones that come back is subtracted, before the ceiling on spend is set.
  • Every segment is judged against that same returns-adjusted number, so the verdict can say brand pays back while non-brand does not, instead of one blended pass or fail.
  • When brand demand covers the whole target the non-brand block short-circuits to zero before any division happens, so an empty non-brand field can never divide by zero, and the spread collapses to a single number.

Rounding happens at display time only. All the arithmetic runs in full precision, so nothing drifts between the panel, the spread and the rows above.

How to read it

Why one blended cost per click hides the only split that matters

A Google account holds two different businesses under one budget line. Someone typing your brand name has already decided; they saw the jacket on a friend, or on your feed, and they came looking for you. That click is cheap, it converts at a rate nothing else in the account touches, and the number of those clicks available is fixed by how many people searched. Someone typing "merino base layer women" has decided nothing. That click costs several times more, converts at a fraction of the rate, and there is effectively no ceiling on how many of them you can buy. Average the two into one cost per click and one conversion rate, multiply by your order target, and the budget that falls out is wrong in a direction you cannot see. This page prints how wrong, on your own inputs, as its last two rows.

The split starts with the ceiling. Brand searches times the share you win as clicks times your brand conversion rate is the most orders brand can ever deliver in a month. If your target sits under that number, the whole plan is a brand campaign and the non-brand fields never get used. If your target sits above it, and for most growing apparel brands it does, the calculator names the exact shortfall and prices every remaining order at non-brand rates. That is where budgets get made or wrecked, because the two segments do not cost anything like the same amount per customer.

Where returns enter the arithmetic

Returns never touch the media cost. They touch what an order is worth, which is what sets the ceiling on the media cost. Contribution per order gets multiplied by the share of orders that stick, one minus your return rate, and then the cost of handling the ones that come back is subtracted, counted on the share that comes back. What is left is the returns-adjusted contribution one order actually delivers. Multiply that by the order target and you get the most this account can spend on Google in a month and still be even at the end of order one. The page then compares each segment's cost per customer against that same figure, which is why the verdict can read that brand pays back while non-brand does not, rather than one blended pass or fail that hides both facts.

Apparel is where the gap bites hardest. Sizing sends a large share of orders back, so contribution per order is thinner than the invoice suggests, and the ceiling drops with it. A non-brand cost per customer that looks survivable against gross contribution can sit well above the returns-adjusted figure. Run the return rate up four points and watch which segment falls out of the plan first: it is never brand.

What Performance Max and Shopping do to the split

Performance Max and Shopping both serve on queries that carry your brand name and on queries that do not, then report one cost per order across the mix. Folded together, a campaign can look efficient while the efficiency is coming entirely from people who already knew you. This calculator folds those campaigns into the non-brand side, because the question it answers is how much it costs to reach a customer who has not decided yet. If you want the split to be accurate, pull a brand and non-brand segmentation from the account, put your brand terms into the brand fields and everything else into non-brand, and treat a Performance Max cost per order that is close to your brand cost per order as a signal that the campaign is harvesting demand rather than creating it.

Three ways this gets entered wrong

The first is entering the account-wide conversion rate into the non-brand field. That number is pulled up by the brand campaign, sometimes by a lot, and using it makes non-brand look cheap enough to fund a target it cannot reach. Pull the rate from a non-brand campaign or from a filtered view, never from the account summary.

The second is reading brand search volume off a keyword tool without checking what it counted. Tools report a head term, sometimes a whole cluster of misspellings and product names, sometimes navigational queries that were going to land on your site regardless. That figure sets the ceiling on the cheapest orders in the plan, so an inflated one quietly shifts orders out of the expensive half of the budget and understates what the month costs.

The third is entering a share of brand searches you can win as clicks that assumes an ad wins every one of them. Published branded benchmarks sit well above non-branded ones, and the quoted figures further up this page put a reasonable band around it. Organic listings still take a large share of clicks on your own name, which is a separate question this page does not answer: whether to bid on your own brand at all. The published study quoted above measures what happens to organic clicks when a paid result appears on a branded query, and it is worth reading before you decide how hard to bid on a term you might already own.

What the range means and what to do with the answer

Only the non-brand budget flexes in the spread, because the non-brand conversion rate is the single number in this model nobody has measured well enough. Brand cost per click and brand conversion rate come out of a campaign that has been running for months, so ranging them would widen the answer without telling you anything. The pessimistic figure is the same plan with the non-brand rate 20% worse, the optimistic one is it 20% better, and brand sits still in both.

Once the number is in front of you, there are three moves. Raise the brand ceiling, which is a demand problem solved outside Google. Lower the non-brand cost per customer, which is a creative, landing page and conversion rate problem. Or lower the target, which is the honest answer when the whole spread sits above what the returns-adjusted contribution can carry. The one move this page will not endorse is funding the gap from a blended cost per click that never existed in the auction.

Questions we get

Five things founders ask about this math

Why split brand and non-brand instead of using one blended cost per click?
Because the auction never blended them. Brand clicks are cheap, convert at a much higher rate, and are capped by how many people searched for you. Non-brand clicks cost more, convert at a fraction of the rate, and have no ceiling. A blended model spreads the cheap segment across orders it can never reach, and the last two rows of the math card print exactly how far off that puts the budget on your own numbers.
Where do Performance Max and Shopping belong in this split?
On the non-brand side. Both serve on branded and unbranded queries and report one cost per order across the mix, so a campaign can look efficient while the efficiency comes from people who already knew the brand. If a Performance Max cost per order sits close to your brand cost per order, treat that as a sign it is harvesting existing demand rather than creating new demand, and price the rest of your target at a real non-brand rate.
Should I be bidding on my own brand name at all?
This calculator assumes you are, and prices those clicks at whatever you enter. Whether it is worth it is a separate question, and the published study quoted above measures the part most people skip: what a paid result does to the organic clicks on the same branded query. Read the three cases in that quote before you decide how hard to bid, because part of the brand traffic in this plan may be traffic you already had.
What do I enter if I have no reliable non-brand conversion rate?
Do not use the account-wide rate. It is pulled up by the brand campaign and it will make non-brand look cheap enough to fund a target it cannot reach. Either pull the rate from a non-brand campaign, or run the calculator twice a few tenths of a point apart and see whether the verdict changes. If the whole spread sits above what your returns-adjusted contribution can carry, the answer does not depend on the guess.
Why does the budget compare against a returns-adjusted number instead of contribution?
Because a returned order refunds the revenue and leaves the acquisition cost behind. Contribution per order is multiplied by the share of orders that stick, then the handling cost of the ones that come back is subtracted, and the result is what one order really leaves you. That figure times your order target is the most this account can spend on Google and still be even at the end of order one, and every segment on the page is judged against it.
