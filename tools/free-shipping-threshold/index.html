How to read it

Why a threshold that grows your AOV can still lose money

A free shipping threshold works on a simple mechanism: a shopper sitting a few dollars under the line adds something to clear it, because paying a shipping charge on the order already in the cart feels worse than adding a little more product and shipping free. That mechanism is well documented. UPS has found that roughly a third of online shoppers add items to a cart specifically to hit a free shipping cutoff, and Baymard's ongoing abandonment research puts extra costs, shipping among them, as one of the leading reasons a cart is abandoned at checkout in the first place. The threshold is a real lever on average order value, and it carries a cost that only shows up after the order ships.

Apparel is where this mechanism gets expensive, and it is the case the defaults on this page are built around. The item added to clear a threshold is rarely the item the shopper came for. It is a second size bought to avoid a guess, a color picked without trying it on, or a small add-on grabbed purely to make the number work. The National Retail Federation puts the online return rate at 19.3% for 2025, quoted in full in the panel above, and apparel specifically runs well above that. The item that exists only to clear a threshold sits at the sharp end of whatever your baseline already is, because it was never really wanted. That is the behaviour this calculator calls the bracketing return rate, and it is the whole reason a threshold that lifts AOV on your dashboard can still cost you money at the bank.

Where the return enters the arithmetic twice

The bracketing return touches this calculation in two separate places, and both stay visible on this page. First, the incremental revenue: only the kept share of the added item counts, so the gross lift is multiplied by one minus the bracketing return rate before any margin is taken on it. Second, the handling cost: every returning item still has to be received, inspected and repacked, and that cost is counted on the expected share of qualifying orders that come back. Shipping is deliberately left out of that second discount. You gave the shipping away the moment the cart cleared the threshold, and you do not get it back when the extra item does.

Contribution margin is the third number doing real work here, and it is worth being precise about what it already contains. It is entered net of product and fulfilment cost, which means the cost of goods on the returned item is already folded into the margin rate rather than pulled out separately the way a full profit-per-order calculation would. That is a deliberate simplification for a tool that is pricing one incremental item, not a whole order, and it is why this page does not ask for a resellable share the way a broader profit calculator does.

The naive number and the real one

A calculator with no returns line takes the gross lift, applies the margin, subtracts the shipping subsidy, and stops. That naive number is printed on this page next to the real one, and the gap between them is labelled the bracketing leak. At a high enough bracketing return rate, the leak is large enough to flip a threshold from a plan that looks obviously good on a naive read to one that quietly costs money on the real one. The gap is the entire portion of the return behaviour that a returns-blind tool cannot see, because it never asked whether the item that got the cart across the line was one the shopper actually intended to keep.

Reach is the dial people get wrong

The reach percentage is meant to capture only the orders whose behaviour the threshold actually changes: carts that would have checked out below the line and grew to clear it. An order that was already going to sit above the threshold is not one the threshold changed, and pricing it as if the threshold caused it overstates the upside. The conservative read goes the other direction. Because you absorb the shipping on every order above the threshold whether the threshold changed anything or not, folding those orders into reach gives you the fuller cost picture even though it understates the revenue attribution. Which read to use depends on what question you are answering: the causal lift from the threshold, or the total shipping bill it now carries.

The number to watch is the break-even lift

The break-even lift is the minimum amount a qualifying cart has to grow by before the threshold pays for itself, given your margin and your bracketing return rate. It is the number that turns a guess into a decision. If your honest estimate of how much carts will grow sits comfortably above the break-even figure, the threshold has room to work even if the estimate is a little optimistic. If the estimate sits below it, or if the whole pessimistic-to-optimistic spread stays negative, no amount of hoping fixes that. The levers to pull are the threshold itself, the margin behind it, and the bracketing return rate the product and fit content on that page are driving.

Run the number twice before setting a threshold live: once at the multiplier you were about to pick, and once at a multiplier a notch higher. On thin-margin apparel with a bracketing return rate anywhere near 30 percent, the gap between those two runs is usually the difference between a threshold that pays for itself and one that quietly subsidizes returns you never see on the P&L until the reconciliation lands.

Questions we get

Five things founders ask about this math

Why does a threshold that raises average order value still cost money?
Because the shopper who adds the extra item to clear the threshold often sends it back. The added revenue is real for exactly as long as the parcel is in transit. Once the bracketing return rate is applied, what actually lands is the kept share of that extra item, and the margin on it has to cover the return handling cost and the shipping you gave away for free. At a high enough bracketing rate, the math flips negative even though the average order value on your dashboard went up.
What does the reach percentage really measure, and how do I estimate mine?
It is the share of orders whose behaviour the threshold actually changes: carts that would have checked out below the threshold and grew to clear it. Pull it from a before-and-after order value distribution around the threshold price point. The note under the field also tells you to fold in orders that were already above the threshold if you want the conservative read, since you absorb their shipping too even though the threshold changed nothing about what they bought.
Why is the bracketing return rate usually higher than my overall return rate?
The item added to clear a threshold was rarely on the shopping list. It was picked to hit a number, often a second size or a color the shopper was unsure about. The National Retail Federation's 19.3% online return rate for 2025, quoted with its link in the published figures panel on this page, is the general baseline, and apparel runs well above it. The item added purely to qualify for free shipping tends to sit at the upper end of whatever your baseline is, because it was never really wanted.
What does the break-even lift number tell me to do next?
It is the minimum dollar amount a qualifying cart has to grow by before the threshold pays for itself, given your margin and your bracketing return rate. Compare it against the lift you actually expect. If your honest estimate sits below the break-even number, the threshold is priced too low for your margin and your return rate. The fix is to raise the threshold, tighten the bracketing return rate, or both.
Why doesn't this tool ask about the resellable share of the returned item?
Because this page prices the incremental item bought only to clear the threshold, run through your contribution margin, which already nets out product and fulfilment cost on the revenue you keep. Splitting a resellable share out again would count the same cost twice. Resellable share belongs on the true profit per order tool, because that one prices the whole order rather than the single item a threshold persuades someone to add.
