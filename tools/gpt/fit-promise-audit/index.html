Where does your fit promise contradict itself?

Find every place your size chart, your product copy, your returns policy and your exchange flow contradict each other, using nothing beyond copy-paste off your own site.

The prompts

Five prompts, run in order, in one ChatGPT chat

You paste five documents off your own site once, then run the five prompts in one ChatGPT chat in this order, because each answer is the input to the next one. Every prompt is printed in full, and the copy button puts it on your clipboard.

01

Read the documents, quote nothing yet

Reads the four documents, inventories them, and names the promises your copy makes that your chart carries no column to verify.

You are auditing the fit promise on my apparel store. Four documents describe how my clothes fit,
written at different times by different people. Your job across this chat is to find where they
contradict each other.

STANDING RULES for every line you write in this chat, from this message to the last one:
- Quote only text I pasted. Never paraphrase a document line and present it as a quote.
- Never state a garment measurement I did not paste. Sleeve length, bicep, armhole, cuff, inseam,
  rise, shoulder, model height, fabric stretch and shrinkage all count.
- Never say a garment runs small, runs large, runs long or runs short unless a document I pasted
  says so in those words. If a customer note says it, quote the note and name the source.
- Anything missing is written as [NEEDS: <the thing>] on its own, with no figure beside it. That is
  the convention the RISE DTC AI Kit uses and this chat uses it too.
- Plain markdown only, no LaTeX. No "it's not X, it's Y" phrasing.
- Zero em dashes, including inside table cells and inside code blocks. When a line reaches for one,
  end the sentence and start a new one, or use a colon. Inside a table cell, use a full stop or a
  semicolon. Write a label, then a colon, then the figure. Never write a label, a dash, then the
  figure.
- Put no dash between a quote and what you say about it. Put the quote on its own line and put your
  sentence on the next line.
- Never use these words: leverage, seamless, robust, elevate, unlock, delve, streamline, empower,
  game-changer, transformative.

Read the documents below and return only this, nothing else:
1. An inventory: for each document, its name, its date if I gave you one, and how many lines it has.
2. The measurement columns my size chart actually carries, listed by name.
3. The garment attributes my product copy makes a promise about, listed by name, each with the SKU
   that promised it.
4. One line: which attributes appear in list 3 and are absent from list 2.

Do not rule on anything yet. Do not suggest a fix.

--- MY DOCUMENTS ---
[paste your size chart, your PDP fit sentences, your policy, your top 3 return reasons, and your
support questions if you have them]
Paste this under it
  • Your size chart, the whole table with its column headers, from your size guide page.
  • The fit sentence from 5 to 8 product pages, each with its SKU, copied exactly as written.
  • Your returns and exchange policy, full text, from your policy page.
  • Your top 3 return reasons as your portal tags them, with row counts, from Shopify, Analytics, Returns, or your returns app. Export the CSV and count rows off it, or attach the export in the chat.
  • Optional: the fit questions your support inbox gets most. Unranked and uncounted is fine, say so.
Everything above is copy-paste off your own site.
02

The contradiction table

Pairs your own lines against each other, two verbatim quotes per row, and names the return reason tag each contradiction lands under.

Now build the contradiction table. Every row needs two verbatim quotes side by side. A row with one
quote is not a finding and does not go in the table.

Columns, in this order and with this wording:

  | # | Document A says (verbatim) | Document B says (verbatim) | Which one the customer believes | What it costs |

Rules for the table, all five binding:
- Both quotes come from my paste, word for word, inside quotation marks, each with its source named
  (document name plus SKU where it is a PDP line).
- "Which one the customer believes" is one sentence naming the document a customer reads at the
  moment of choosing a size, and why that one wins. A customer picks a size on the product page.
- "What it costs" names the return reason tag the contradiction lands under, using my own tag
  wording. If I gave you counts, put the count there. If I did not, write [NEEDS: return count by
  reason].
- A promise with nothing to check it against is a contradiction. Quote the promise, and in column
  B write the chart column that would verify it as [NEEDS: <column>].
- Where a fit sentence tells a customer to change size, quote it, then quote both chart rows a
  customer moves between when they follow it, and name every measurement that moves with them.
  A size-change instruction that mentions one measurement while my chart moves two is a
  contradiction and it earns a row.
- Never invent a measurement to prove a row. If a row needs a number I did not paste, the row still
  runs and the number stays as [NEEDS: <the number>].

Under the table, list every contradiction you considered and dropped, with the reason you dropped
it in five words or fewer.

04

The policy read, exchange first

Quotes every cost, delay and step your policy puts in front of a size swap, then rewrites your exchange paragraph so a swap costs less than a refund.

Now audit my returns and exchange policy against the exchange share I gave you.

1. Quote every line in my policy that puts a cost, a delay, or a step in front of a customer who
   wants a different size. One quote per line, with no paraphrase.

2. For each quoted line, write one sentence on the choice it pushes a customer toward at that
   moment. Use my own numbers where I gave them. Where the arithmetic runs on a figure I did not
   paste, write [NEEDS: <the figure>] and carry on.

3. Quote any line in my policy that describes a store I am not. A line about a charge I never make
   or a step I do not run belongs here, with the line from my own paste that contradicts it.

4. If I gave you the cost of handling one return and the fee my policy charges, print the gap per
   return and the monthly gap, with the arithmetic on the line. If I gave you only one of the two,
   write [NEEDS: <the missing one>] and print nothing.

5. Rewrite my exchange paragraph so that a customer who wants a different size pays less than a
   customer who wants their money back. Change the fee on the exchange path only, and print the
   new figure in the paragraph. Hold my window, my process steps and my refund-path fee exactly as
   I published them. Under the paragraph, print the cost of the change in dollars: my exchanges
   per month times the fee I gave up, with the arithmetic. Mark any figure you had to leave blank
   as [NEEDS: <the figure>]. This is the only writing you do in this chat, and it is one paragraph.

[trimmed: two more quote-and-sentence pairs, on the printing step and the carrier trip.]

"We charge a flat $8 label fee, deducted from your refund or from your exchange." A customer deciding whether to fix a size problem must weigh $8 against keeping the wrong item.

"This applies to returns and to exchanges." A customer choosing between a refund and an exchange faces the same $8 on both paths, leaving no price reason to choose the exchange over the refund; 28% of 348 monthly returns become exchanges.

"Choose your new size in the portal." A customer must commit to a specific replacement size at the moment of initiating the return, before the store can confirm that size is available.

"We ship the new item once the original is scanned by the carrier." A customer cannot receive the replacement until they have dropped off the return and the carrier has scanned it, so the new item's ship date depends entirely on when they act on the return.

"The $8 fee applies." A customer completing the exchange flow sees the fee again at the final confirmation step, creating a last moment to choose a refund instead.
