How to read it

What the dashboard AOV leaves out

Average order value is one of the simplest numbers in ecommerce and one of the easiest to get wrong. Divide revenue by orders and you have gross AOV, the number every dashboard shows on the home screen. The problem is what "orders" counts. An order placed on Monday and refunded on the following Tuesday still counts as an order in that division, and its full price still counts as revenue. Only when the refund posts does the money actually leave, usually days or weeks after the order shipped and the AOV was already reported. Net AOV is the same order count divided by the revenue that actually stayed, and in apparel the gap between the two is rarely small.

Apparel is the hard case because sizing gets decided at home, not at checkout. A shopper who orders a small and a medium to keep one has placed one order and bought two units, and the calendar has already turned before the return comes back. Your morning dashboard reports the gross order. Your bank account reports the refund a few weeks later. Both are true, and only one of them pays your bills.

Two ways to enter the refund side, one number underneath

This calculator gives you two routes into the same math, and both collapse onto a single refund fraction so they can never quietly disagree. The first route is a real refund total for the period, read straight off Shopify or your payment processor. That number is used exactly as entered, including a real zero. The second route is a return rate and an exchange share, used only when the refund field is left blank. An exchange keeps the revenue and swaps the size or color, so it never becomes a refund dollar, and the exchange share is what carves those orders out of the return rate before the refund estimate runs. Whichever route you take, everything downstream, net AOV, the gap, the contribution math, reads from the same one fraction.

Where the $10 goes before it reaches contribution

Most AOV projections multiply the lift by contribution margin and stop there. Ten dollars at 45% margin reads as four dollars and fifty cents of extra profit, and that is the number a returns-blind spreadsheet will show you. It is wrong for the same reason gross AOV is wrong: the extra dollars sit inside the same basket as the rest of the order, and they refund at the same rate. If eighteen percent of the basket comes back, eighteen percent of the extra ten dollars comes back with it. What is left, after both the margin and the refund rate have had their say, is the real number this page calls the contribution gain per order. Multiply it across every order in the period and you get the total contribution gain, which is what the lift is actually worth to the business rather than what it looks like on a slide.

Reading the overstatement line

The math card also prints what a returns-blind tool would have promised for the same lift, and the difference between that number and the real one is labeled overstatement. Overstatement is the exact share of the projected gain that refunds will take back before it reaches a bank account, and it grows or shrinks with the same return rate that drives the rest of this page. At the example loaded into the fields above, refunds run $61,200 against $340,000 of gross revenue, so a returns-blind projection of $22,500 in extra contribution becomes $18,450, and the $4,050 difference is the overstatement line. Drop the refund share and that line shrinks toward nothing, which makes a naive projection close enough to work from. Push the refund share up and the line starts to rival the real gain, which is the clearest possible sign that any AOV plan built on gross numbers needs a returns adjustment before anyone signs off on the ad spend behind it.

Why contribution per order is the number to watch

AOV on its own does not pay for anything. Contribution per order does, and it is what is left after the product, the shipping, the processing and the other variable costs, before you pay to acquire the customer. This page prices the lift on contribution because that is the number that funds acquisition spend, repeat purchase economics and everything else downstream. The extra dollars a lift buys you, net of refunds, are also the extra room you can spend chasing the customer who placed that order, which is why the same figure doubles as acquisition headroom in the panel above. Chasing AOV on its own can hand you a bigger dashboard number while a return-heavy basket leaves the bank balance where it was. Pricing the lift on contribution keeps the return rate inside the plan instead of leaving it as a surprise at month end.

What actually moves this number

Three dials move the verdict on this page. The return rate, because every point of it eats into the lift before it ever reaches contribution. The exchange share, because it is the one lever that recovers revenue without giving up a sale. And the lift target itself, which is the only number here that is entirely a plan rather than a measurement. Run the same lift at your real return rate and again five points lower, and compare the gap in total contribution gain against what a size guide or a fit page might realistically move. On an apparel order book, closing the return rate is usually worth more than chasing the next AOV point.

Questions we get

Five things founders ask about this math

Why does my dashboard AOV differ from the net AOV on this page?
Your dashboard divides revenue by orders the moment the order is placed, before any refunds have posted. This page runs the same division after the refunds you enter or estimate have been taken out. Both numbers are correct for what they measure. Gross AOV tells you what customers agreed to pay. Net AOV tells you what actually stayed.
Should I enter a real refund total or use the return rate estimate?
Use the real total whenever you have it, straight off Shopify or your payment processor, and leave the switch on. Switch it off only when you do not have that number yet, and the return rate plus the exchange share below will estimate it for you. A real number is always tighter than an estimate.
What counts as an exchange rather than a refund?
An exchange swaps the size, the color or the item and keeps the original sale on the books, so no refund dollars leave and it should not be counted in your return rate the way a full refund is. This calculator removes the exchange share from the return rate before it estimates refund dollars, so a store that handles a lot of exchanges does not get penalized twice.
Why is a $10 AOV lift worth less than $10 in contribution?
The extra dollars from a lift sit inside the same basket as the rest of the order and refund at the same rate. At a margin of 45% and a refund rate of 18%, ten dollars of lift is worth three dollars and sixty nine cents in contribution, not four dollars and fifty cents. The math card shows both numbers so you can see exactly where the difference goes.
What does the overstatement line mean?
It is the gap between what a returns-blind projection would have promised for this lift and what the lift is actually worth once refunds are taken out. A small overstatement means your return rate is low enough that a naive projection is close to safe. A large one means any AOV plan built on gross numbers needs the returns adjustment before it goes in front of whoever signs off on the spend behind it.
