  • The weights sum to exactly 100, so the weighted total is already the score. There is no normalising step and no hidden multiplier.
  • The score measures presence only. A live flow with one badly written email scores the same as a great one. This tool checks whether the flow exists, and nothing else.
  • Partial counts as half the weight, which is a blunt instrument on purpose. The band above shows you what the blunt instrument costs you.
  • Eight flows are in scope. Birthday, quiz result, replenishment and the rest are left out rather than padded in to inflate the count.
  • Returns enter the weighting, not the arithmetic. Post-purchase and back in stock both carry weight partly because they prevent returns. No money figure is produced here, so no return rate is asked for.
  • Ties are broken by a fixed flow order, so the three gaps listed above are the same three every time you load the page with the same answers.

Rounding happens at display time only. All the arithmetic runs in full precision, so nothing drifts between the panel, the spread and the rows above.

How to read it

Why the eight flows do not count equally

Most flow checklists hand you a fraction. Five of eight built, so you are at sixty-something percent, and the number treats a VIP flow and an abandoned cart flow as the same object. In an apparel store they are nowhere near the same object. One fires on a shopper who reached checkout with a size selected and a card half entered. The other fires on a small group of repeat buyers to protect a relationship that already exists. Both are worth building. Only one of them is worth building this week.

This scorecard weights each flow by purchase intent at the moment it fires, multiplied by how many people trigger it. Abandoned cart takes the top weight because it catches the highest intent moment email ever gets. Welcome sits just behind it, because it is the only flow every single new subscriber sees and it owns first-purchase conversion, so its audience dwarfs everything else on the list. Post-purchase takes more than a token score because in apparel it carries fit and care guidance, which lifts repeat rate and deflects returns at the same time: two results from one flow. Browse abandonment sits mid-table, a larger audience than cart at materially lower intent. Winback earns a middle weight because it recovers people who already bought, the cheapest revenue on the list, with a payoff that arrives late.

The two weights that look wrong until you read them

Sunset is the deliberate call. It produces almost no direct revenue, and an unweighted checklist and a revenue-only model would both score it near zero. It carries real weight here because it protects the inbox placement of the other seven flows. Keep mailing subscribers who stopped opening months ago and your welcome series starts landing in Promotions, your cart reminders start landing in spam, and the flows you did build quietly stop working. Scoring sunset near zero is exactly the mistake a flat checklist makes, in the opposite direction.

Back in stock earns its weight for an apparel-specific reason. A restock alert that names the size the shopper wanted converts demand you already paid to create. It also prevents a specific expensive event: the shopper who settles for the wrong size because the right one was gone, then sends it back. That is the second place returns enter this scorecard, and neither place asks you for a return rate, because this tool produces no money figure. If you want the money version, the email ROI and email list value tools both take returns as an input.

What partial means, and why the band is always shown

Partial is the honest answer for a flow that exists as one email with a generic discount, or a flow that was built two years ago and has not been opened since. It counts for half the weight. Half is a blunt instrument, so the page never hides what the bluntness costs. The band recomputes your score twice: once with every partial demoted to absent, once with every partial promoted to live. A brand with four half-built flows sees a wide band, and that width is the signal. It means the real number depends on how generous you were being with the word partial, and the fix is to look at those flows again rather than to trust the headline.

The flat checklist number sits right next to the weighted one

Both numbers print, on purpose. The flat coverage figure is the count of flows divided by eight, which is what an unweighted checklist would have told you. The weighted score is the same answers priced by what each flow carries. When the weighted score comes in higher, you own the flows that matter and the gaps are at the edges. When it comes in lower, you have built the easy flows and skipped the two that pay for the rest. The distance between the two figures is the whole argument for weighting, printed in your own numbers rather than asserted.

What the top three gaps are for

The panel ranks every missing flow by the points it is costing you, then names the three largest. A flow marked partial still shows up there, carrying half its weight, which is how a half-built welcome series can outrank a completely missing VIP flow. Each gap comes with a plain sentence about who is going unserved and when, and no revenue estimate at all. Any number that claimed to know what your missing browse abandonment flow would earn would be invented, and this page prints no invented numbers.

One caution before you act on the score. It counts whether a flow is live and stops there. A live flow with three thin emails and a stale discount code scores identically to a well built one. A brand can reach a high score and still be leaving money on the table inside flows that technically run. Treat the score as the first pass: build what is missing, in the order the points suggest, then go back and read what the live ones actually say to a shopper who is standing in front of a mirror with two sizes.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this scorecard

Why weight the flows instead of counting them equally?
Because an unweighted count says five of eight and stops there, whether the five you own are the two that carry the revenue or the five that were easiest to switch on. Omnisend measures welcome and abandoned cart separately from every other automated send, and reports that abandoned cart, welcome and browse abandonment together account for most automated orders. Weighting by revenue criticality is what turns a count into a priority list. The flat coverage figure still prints beside the weighted score, so you can see the difference on your own answers.
What counts as partial rather than live?
Partial is the right answer when the flow technically exists but is not doing the job: one email where the flow needs three, a generic discount with no product context, a sequence built years ago that nobody has opened since, or a flow that is switched on for a segment that barely triggers. Live means it fires for the whole eligible audience and someone has read it this quarter. If you are unsure, mark it partial and read the band, which shows you the score both ways.
Does this work if I am not on Klaviyo?
Yes. Klaviyo, Omnisend, Attentive and every other ecommerce email platform run the same eight flows under different names, so the scorecard is platform agnostic. Klaviyo appears on this page only because it is the term most apparel founders search for. This page is not affiliated with Klaviyo.
Why does sunset carry weight when it sells almost nothing?
Because it protects the inbox placement of the seven flows that do sell. Keep mailing subscribers who stopped engaging and the whole sending reputation degrades, which pushes your welcome series into Promotions and your cart reminders into spam. Scoring it near zero would price it by direct revenue alone and miss what it is actually for, which is the same failure as counting every flow equally, in the opposite direction.
Where are birthday, quiz result and replenishment flows?
Out of scope on purpose. They are worth building for some apparel brands and worth nothing for others, and adding them would pad the denominator so every score drifts down for a reason that has nothing to do with the eight flows that carry revenue. This tool scores the eight that carry it. If you run a strong birthday flow, count it as a bonus rather than trying to fit it into a slot here.
My score is high. Does that mean my email is in good shape?
It means the structure is in place. The score only reflects whether each flow is running, so a flow with three thin emails scores the same as a well built one. Read a high score as permission to move from building to improving: open each live flow, read it as a shopper deciding between two sizes, and check whether it does more than repeat the discount from the welcome series.
