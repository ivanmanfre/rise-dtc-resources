  • The break-even ceiling runs on contribution after returns. Contribution is multiplied by the share of orders that stay sold, and the handling cost of the ones that come back is subtracted, so the affordable budget falls as the return rate rises.
  • Every retargeting conversion counts as a new customer at face value. That is generous, and it is one reason the budget range on this page matters more than the single figure.
  • CPM, CTR and conversion rate are held constant across the spend level. The model is linear in budget, so a plan that doubles spend should be rechecked at the CPM that doubled spend actually clears.
  • Repeat orders are out of scope. This is a first-order break-even. The lifetime question belongs to the CPA and payback tools.

How to read it

Why one blended CPM sizes an apparel budget wrong

A Meta budget is a purchase order for orders. You are buying impressions, a share of those impressions become clicks, and a share of those clicks become customers. Multiply the three together and you get the only number that decides the budget: how many orders one dollar of spend buys. Divide your target by that number and you have the month's spend. All of that is arithmetic. The reason budgets still come out wrong is that most planning runs it once, on one blended CPM, one blended CTR and one blended conversion rate, when the account is actually buying from two very different audiences.

Cold prospecting reaches people who have never seen the product. The CPM is usually lower, because the audience is large, and the conversion rate is usually much lower, because the visit is a first meeting. Retargeting reaches a small pool of people who already viewed a product or abandoned a cart. The CPM is usually higher, because the pool is small and everyone is bidding on it, and the conversion rate is far higher, because the decision was already halfway made. Blend those two and the average hides both. The blended cost per order looks reachable while the prospecting half of the account, which is where growth actually comes from, sits well above it.

Retargeting looks cheap because it takes credit for demand you already paid for

A retargeting conversion is a person your prospecting spend, your organic content or your email list already produced. This calculator counts every one of those conversions as a new customer at face value, which is the generous reading, and it caps the retargeting share of the budget at 40% with a notice past 30%. Both guardrails exist for the same reason: the cheaper cost per order in retargeting comes from a pool that prospecting refills. Move budget into retargeting and the cost per order on the panel drops while the pool shrinks under it, which is the shape of a plan that reports well for a month and stalls in the second. The honest use of the retargeting share is to model what you are already doing, then watch what the blended figure does when you set the share to zero.

What returns do to the ceiling

The second half of this page is the break-even check, and it runs on contribution after returns rather than gross contribution. Contribution is multiplied by the share of orders that stay sold, then the handling cost of the returns you will actually get is subtracted. On apparel that gap is large. At a $55 contribution and a 25% return and exchange rate with $10 to process each return, the figure the ceiling is built on is $38.75, roughly 30% below the gross number. Running the same plan on gross contribution would tell an apparel brand it can afford about a third more budget than it can, and that error compounds every month the plan stays in place.

The ceiling itself is your target multiplied by that returns-adjusted contribution. It is the budget at which the contribution from the new customers you bought exactly pays for the spend that bought them, on the first order only. Headroom is the gap between that ceiling and the budget your rates require. Positive headroom means the month pays for itself before any repeat order arrives. Negative headroom means you are buying the first order at a loss and betting on the second, which is a defensible plan when you have measured repeat behavior and a poor one when you have not.

What the campaign consolidations mean for the split

Meta has spent several years pushing accounts toward fewer, larger campaigns, with broad targeting and automated placement, and the shopping campaign types that sit at the top of that push do not hand you a clean prospecting-versus-retargeting split at all. Inside one consolidated campaign the delivery system decides how much of your budget lands on people who already know you. The controls you keep are the existing-customer setting inside those campaigns, exclusion audiences on the campaigns you still run manually, and the split between the consolidated campaign and everything outside it.

That makes pricing the two audiences separately more useful, not less. Pull a breakdown of the last 30 days by audience or by campaign, work out the impressions, clicks and orders each side produced, and enter those. What you get back is the price the account is paying for each kind of customer, and how much of your month's budget is being spent to reach people you already reached. If the consolidated campaign is quietly running at 35% retargeting, the panel says so, and the cost per new customer you thought you were paying moves.

Why the range is the answer, and the single figure is the headline

Conversion rate is the input founders guess hardest and the one the budget is most sensitive to. Budget moves inversely with it: a rate 20% lower needs a budget 25% higher for the same target. So this page flexes both segment rates 20% in each direction and prints the two ends exactly, then draws the break-even ceiling against both. The default state of this page is the interesting one. The plan clears break-even if the rates hold, and loses money if they come in 20% light. That is the real position most apparel plans are in, and it is invisible when the budget is quoted as a single number in a deck.

Three dials move the answer. Conversion rate, which is a product page, sizing guidance and offer question before it is a media question. CPM and CTR, which are creative questions, and where the published figures show every industry paying more each year. And contribution after returns, which is where an apparel brand usually finds the largest single move, because a few points off the return rate raises the ceiling on every order in the month at once.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this budget

Why does the affordable budget use contribution after returns?
Because the orders that come back do not pay for the ads that bought them. The ceiling multiplies your contribution by the share of orders that stay sold, then subtracts what it costs to handle the returns you will get. At a $55 contribution with a 25% return rate and $10 of handling, the ceiling is built on $38.75 per order rather than $55.00. Running it on gross contribution would size an apparel budget roughly a third too high.
Why is the retargeting share capped at 40%?
Retargeting converts a pool that prospecting refills, so its cheap cost per order is partly borrowed from spend you already made. Above 40% of the budget the model stops being a plan for finding new customers, so the field stops there and anything higher is clamped before the math runs. Past 30% the panel prints a notice that you are paying to reach people you already reached. The cap is a guardrail on the model, and your account can still be told to do anything you want.
Meta runs one consolidated campaign for me. How do I split prospecting and retargeting?
Pull the last 30 days broken out by audience or by campaign, and count impressions, clicks and orders on each side. Enter those as the two segments and the retargeting share as the portion of spend that landed on people who already knew you. Inside a consolidated campaign you do not set that split directly, which is exactly why it is worth measuring: the existing-customer setting and your exclusion audiences are the only controls, and the panel tells you what the current mix is costing per new customer.
Why does the budget move so much when I change conversion rate by a few tenths?
Budget is inversely proportional to conversion rate. Going from 2.2% to 1.76%, a drop of 20%, raises the budget needed for the same target by 25%. That is why this page prints the range instead of a single figure, and why both ends are checked against the break-even ceiling. If the plan clears at one end and loses at the other, the next step is measuring the rate, not moving the budget.
Does this count repeat orders?
No. This is a first-order break-even, so the ceiling only counts the contribution of the order you just bought. A brand with measured repeat behavior can afford to spend past this ceiling on purpose, and the CPA and payback tools are where that case gets modelled. Starting from the first-order number keeps the decision honest, because repeat rates are usually assumed rather than measured.
What CPM, CTR and conversion rate should I enter?
Your own, pulled per segment from the last 30 days. The published figures quoted on this page are blended account averages across prospecting and retargeting together, so they sanity-check your entry rather than replace it. If you have no split yet, run the page once at a zero retargeting share to price cold traffic alone, then again with your real mix, and compare the two cost-per-order figures.
