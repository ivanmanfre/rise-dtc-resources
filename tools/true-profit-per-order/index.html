How to read it

Why a returned order costs more than it refunds

An order that comes back takes the revenue with it and leaves most of the cost behind. The carrier was already paid to move the parcel. The processor already took its percentage on the gross transaction and it keeps the fixed fee. The ad that bought the customer already cleared. What arrives back at the warehouse is a physical item that has to be received, inspected and repacked, and only part of the inventory value comes home with it. That gap is why this calculator asks for a resellable share rather than assuming every return goes straight back on the shelf at full price.

Apparel is the hard case, and it is the case these defaults are built around. Sizing gets decided at home, in front of a mirror, after the parcel has already moved. A shopper who orders two sizes to keep one has bought two units and paid for one. Your morning dashboard records the gross order. Your bank account records the refund a week later, minus the fees that never came back, plus the label you paid to bring the second size home. The same order reads healthy in the daily report and thin in the month-end reconciliation, and the whole difference sits in the returns line.

The four places returns enter the arithmetic

Returns show up four separate times on one order, and this page keeps all four visible. First, refunded revenue: only the kept share of orders leaves money behind, so revenue is multiplied by one minus the return rate. Second, product cost: you recover inventory value only on the resellable share, so a return that cannot be resold charges you for the goods a second time. Third, return handling: the label, the inspection and the repack, counted on the expected share of orders that come back. Fourth, card fees: charged on the gross order and modelled as kept by the processor on a refund, which is what published card pricing describes.

Outbound shipping is deliberately left at full weight on returned orders. Reducing it in proportion to the return rate would flatter the answer by pretending a parcel can be un-shipped. You paid the carrier when the box left, and that money is gone whatever the customer decides.

Resellable share is the dial most people skip

When a garment comes back worn, unfolded, without tags, or in a season that has already turned over, it stops being A-grade inventory. It moves to markdown, to an outlet channel, or to a write-off. At 80% resellable, one fifth of everything that comes back charges you the landed product cost twice: once when you bought it, once when you cannot sell it at the price you planned. Most spreadsheets fold that into an average and lose sight of it. This one gives it a line of its own so you can watch what happens when you drag the dial from 80% down to 50%.

Three things people get wrong on the returns-adjusted view

The first is entering the CAC the ad platform reports instead of the blended one. The platform counts the orders it believes it caused. Your bank account counts every dollar you spent against every new customer you actually got, and the gap between those two numbers is usually the reason an order that looks profitable on screen is not profitable at the end of the month.

The second is reading the return rate off a short window during a month of growth. Returns lag the sale by weeks. When order volume is climbing, a trailing 30 day return rate divides last month's returns by this month's larger order count, which pushes the rate down and pushes the profit up. Pull the rate over a window long enough for the returns to land, or the calculator will hand you back an optimistic number in your own handwriting.

The third is leaving the resellable share at 100%. It almost never is, and setting it there quietly removes an entire cost from the model.

The number to watch is contribution before acquisition

Contribution before acquisition is what one order leaves you before you pay to find the customer, and it sets the hard ceiling on what you can ever spend to acquire one. When it comes in below your CAC, order one loses money and repeat purchases become the only route to a customer who pays for themselves. That is what the break-even orders line counts: how many orders one customer has to place before the acquisition cost is repaid. When contribution before acquisition falls to zero or below, no number of orders repays it, and the page says so instead of printing a large integer that implies patience will fix it.

Once you have the answer, there are three dials that move it. The return rate, which sizing guidance, fit content and product photography all touch. The resellable share, which is a returns-handling and grading question more than a marketing one. And the acquisition cost, which is where most operators start and where the smallest gains usually are. Run the same order at a 22% return rate and again at 18%, and compare that swing against what a 10% cut in CAC does. On apparel economics the returns dial almost always wins.

Questions we get

Six things founders ask about this math

Why are card fees still charged when the order is refunded?
Card processors charge on the gross transaction, and on standard published pricing the percentage and the fixed fee stay with the processor when you refund the customer. Stripe lists 2.9% plus 30 cents per successful transaction. Shopify Payments publishes a range from 2.5% to 2.9% plus 30 cents depending on plan. This calculator applies the fee to every order shipped and never credits it back, which is the conservative reading.
Why does outbound shipping stay in the math for returned orders?
The carrier has already been paid. The parcel went out, the label was bought, the cost cleared. Reducing outbound shipping in proportion to returns would flatter the answer by pretending you can un-ship a box. Return handling is counted separately, on the expected share of orders that come back, so the two costs never double up.
What return rate should I enter if I sell apparel?
Pull your own, over a window long enough for returns to land. Returns lag the sale by weeks, so a trailing 30 day rate during a growth month reads low. The published figures quoted on this page cover all retail and all online retail, and they are there to sanity-check the number you enter rather than to replace it. If you have no reliable figure yet, run the calculator twice, a few points apart, and see whether the verdict changes.
What does resellable at full price mean, and how do I estimate it?
It is the share of returned units that can go straight back into A-grade stock and sell at the original price. Count a month of returns and sort them: back on the shelf at full price, sold at markdown, moved to outlet, written off. The first bucket divided by the total is your resellable share. Most apparel operators who count it for the first time find it lower than they assumed, especially on items that ship in peak season and come back after it.
My return rate and my CAC are both estimates. Does that break the answer?
It widens it, which is why the spread is printed above. Both inputs are flexed 20% in each direction to give a pessimistic and an optimistic read of the same order. If the whole spread sits below zero, the estimate has stopped being the problem and the order economics have taken over. If the spread straddles zero, pull the real return rate from Shopify and the real CAC from your bank statement before you decide anything.
Does a healthy repeat rate rescue an order that loses money on the first sale?
Sometimes, and the optional repeat dial is there to tell you which case you are in. Switch it on and the page computes expected orders per customer as 1 divided by 1 minus the repeat rate, then multiplies by contribution before acquisition and subtracts the acquisition cost once. When that total clears zero, the customer pays for themselves even though order one did not, and the verdict moves to FIX FIRST. When contribution before acquisition is zero or negative, repeat orders make the total worse rather than better, because every additional order adds cost.
