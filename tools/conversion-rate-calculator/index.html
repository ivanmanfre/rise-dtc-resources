  • A row counts only when its sessions field is filled in and greater than zero. An empty row never becomes a zero percent row, it simply sits out of the blend.
  • Orders left blank on a row that has sessions filled in are read as zero, not as missing data.
  • Every row needs to come from the same attribution model and the same window. Mixing a session count pulled from one analytics tool with an order count from another invalidates the comparison this page runs.
  • The confidence band uses a Wald normal approximation at 95%, the same method behind every rate on this page. It is an approximation, not an exact binomial interval, and it reads oddly narrow at very small sample sizes.
  • Mix shift changes exactly one input: paid social sessions, up 20%, with every per source rate held exactly where you entered it. Nothing else about the funnel is assumed to move.
  • This page does not model returns. A returned order still counted as a conversion the moment it was placed, so returns leave this arithmetic untouched. cpa-calculator and mer-calculator are where returns start changing the money.

Rounding happens at display time only. All the arithmetic runs in full precision, so nothing drifts between the panel, the spread and the rows above.

How to read it

Why a blended rate hides four different sources

An apparel site running paid social, paid search, email and a direct plus organic bucket is really running four separate small businesses under one dashboard tile. Paid social usually pulls in the widest, coldest audience and posts the lowest rate. Email reaches people who already own something from you and posts the highest. Blend the four together into one line and the number that comes out the other side describes nobody, because it is a weighted average of four very different shoppers at four very different points in a relationship with the brand. The blended rate on this page is the same arithmetic your dashboard runs. What changes is that every source sits next to it, so the average stops hiding the sources that built it.

Why the weight matters more than the count

A source with a low rate and a small share of sessions barely moves the blend. The same low rate carried by your biggest traffic source drags the whole number down with it, and that is usually paid social on an apparel account running cold prospecting at scale. The session share column next to each rate on this page exists for exactly that reason: a rate on its own tells you how a source converts, and the share next to it tells you how much that source is allowed to move your headline number. A source can post a strong rate and still barely matter to the blend if almost nobody lands there, and a weak source can matter enormously if it is where most of the traffic sits.

What the confidence band is measuring

Every rate on this page carries a 95% Wald interval built from the sessions behind it. This is not the same kind of range as flexing a return rate or a CAC estimate twenty percent in either direction, because sessions and orders are counted rather than guessed. The uncertainty here is sampling noise: even a perfectly stable underlying rate produces a slightly different observed rate every time you count a finite number of visits. A source with a hundred thousand sessions has a tight band around its rate. A source with four hundred sessions has a wide one, and reading its point estimate as a fact rather than a range is where a lot of channel decisions go wrong on a smaller apparel account still building traffic in a newer channel.

The few orders warning

Any included row with fewer than 30 orders gets flagged rather than ranked against the others as if it were solid. Thirty orders is not a magic number, it is a floor below which a Wald interval starts behaving badly and a single unusual week can move a rate by a full percentage point or more. A flagged row still shows its rate and still counts toward the blend, because it did happen, but this page will not let it win or lose the spread against a source with real volume behind it. Treat a flagged row as directional until it has collected more traffic, not as a channel decision on its own.

What mix shift is actually showing you

Mix shift answers a narrower question than most of this page: what happens to the blended number if paid social grows twenty percent and every source keeps converting exactly as it does today. Nothing about your site, your offer or your checkout changes in this scenario. Only the traffic mix does. When paid social converts below your blend, growing it pulls the blended rate down even though nothing got worse. This is the mechanic behind a founder watching the site's headline conversion rate slide during a month when the team actually scaled spend correctly and every individual source held its rate steady. The blended number moved because the mix moved, not because anything on the site did.

Where returns fit, and where they do not

This calculator does not ask about returns, on purpose. A conversion is counted the moment the order is placed, and whether that order ships, gets worn, gets returned, or gets exchanged for a different size happens after the moment this page is measuring. An apparel shopper who orders two sizes to keep one still converted twice against these numbers, even though one of those two orders will come back. Returns absolutely change what those orders are worth, but that is a different question, answered by true-profit-per-order and contribution-margin, and a different money question again, answered by cpa-calculator and mer-calculator. Adding a returns field to this page would imply a dollar output this tool does not produce, so it stays out.

Reading the split before you read the headline

The order to read this page in is the split first, the blend second. Look at which source carries the most sessions, look at what that source converts at, and only then look at the blended number at the top, because by then you already know most of what built it. A blended rate that looks flat month over month can hide a paid social rate quietly declining while email keeps propping the average up, and a blended rate that looks like it improved can hide nothing more than a shift toward a channel that already converted better before anything on the funnel changed. The split is the diagnosis. The blended number is the summary you hand to someone who does not have time for the split.

Questions we get

Five things founders ask about this math

Why does my blended conversion rate look nothing like any one of my channels?
Because it is a weighted average of all of them, and the weight is each source's share of sessions, not an equal split. A source with a huge share of traffic and a modest rate can pull the blended number toward it even while a smaller, higher converting source sits right next to it on this page. The blended figure describes the mix, not any single channel inside it.
What does "too few orders to read" mean, and when does it show up?
Any included row with fewer than 30 orders gets that flag. Below that count, the confidence band behaves badly and a single unusual week can swing the rate by a full percentage point or more. The row still counts toward the blend, but this page will not let it win or lose the spread against a source with real volume behind it.
Why do you show a range instead of one confidence number?
Because sessions and orders are counted, not guessed, and even a perfectly stable rate produces a slightly different observed number every time you count a finite sample. The 95% Wald band shown here reflects that sampling noise. A source with a hundred thousand sessions has a tight band. A source with a few hundred has a wide one, and the point estimate alone hides which case you are in.
What is the mix shift number telling me?
It answers one narrow question: what happens to your blended rate if paid social sessions grow 20% and every source keeps converting exactly as it does today. Nothing on the site changes in that scenario, only the traffic mix does. When paid social converts below your blend, growing it can pull the blended number down even though every individual rate held steady, which is exactly the pattern that makes a headline rate look worse in a month spend actually scaled correctly.
Why doesn't this calculator ask about returns?
A conversion here is counted the instant the order is placed, before a return can happen. A returned order still counted as a conversion at that moment, so returns do not change this arithmetic. Adding a returns field would imply a money output this page does not produce. true-profit-per-order and contribution-margin cover what a returned order costs, and cpa-calculator and mer-calculator cover where returns change what an order was worth acquiring.
