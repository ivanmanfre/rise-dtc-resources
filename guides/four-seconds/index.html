RISE DTC / The first four seconds

Why this sheet exists

A page written for regulars, opened by someone who met you today

Written by Mattan Danino, CEO of RISE DTC · 5 August 2026 · No email, no form, nothing gated.

A product page is written for someone who already knows the brand. They read the reviews last week, or they have been meaning to order since March. That page shows the price and gets out of the way, which is the correct thing to do for that person.

Paid traffic sends a different person to the same page. They have never heard of you. They arrived because something in the feed was interesting enough to tap, and now they are four seconds into meeting a brand that is already asking for a card number.

The ads in that situation are usually fine. They were bought to produce a tap and they produced a tap. The gap opens after the tap, on a page that was never written to convince anyone of anything.

Nothing below needs a developer, a new tool, or a login you do not already have. Four checks, in the order we run them, then the arithmetic that tells you what one of those visitors is actually worth to you.

01

Check 01 · the arrival

Click your own ad from your phone

Find the ad in your own feed, on the phone you actually carry, and tap it the way a customer would. A desktop preview inside Ads Manager loads a different page on a different connection, which is how this check gets passed by stores that would fail it.

You are looking for the gap between what the ad promised and what the screen delivers. An ad that sells a solution to sore feet, landing on a grid of twelve colourways, is a different promise arriving in a different room.

Run it

Count out loud from the moment the page paints. At four seconds, stop. Whatever is on the screen at that point is your entire pitch to a cold visitor.

02

Check 02 · the opening line

Read the first screen out loud

Read only what is visible before you scroll, and read it as a sentence you are saying to a person standing in front of you. When we run this, that sentence is often a product name, a number, and the word Add.

Said out loud to a stranger, that is a request for money placed before an introduction. The fix is cheap. One line above the price that says who this is for and what it does, in the words a customer would use.

Run it

Write down the first screen word for word. If a reader could not tell from it what you sell and who it is for, that line goes above the price today.

03

Check 03 · the outside read

Hand your phone to someone who has never heard of you

Anyone outside the business will do. Open the page, hand over the phone, count four seconds, take it back. Then ask what this brand sells, and why they would give it their card.

You already know every answer, which is exactly why you cannot run this check on yourself. The value is in the questions they cannot answer.

Run it

Write down every answer they could not give you. Those gaps are the copy brief for the first screen, in priority order, for free.

04

Check 04 · the other door

Give the ones who came too early somewhere else to go

The visitors who buy on a first visit are a small share of the ones you paid to bring over. Your own analytics will tell you how small.

The rest arrived before they were ready to spend.

A product page offers those people exactly one action, and it is the one they are not ready to take. So they leave, the traffic gets blamed, and the same page gets more budget pointed at it next month.

Give them a second thing to do that costs them nothing. Something genuinely worth reading, or one email that earns the right to send the next one. Ask for the card on the visit after you stop being a stranger.

Run it

Open your analytics and find what share of paid visitors bought on their first session. Whatever the remainder is, that is the size of the audience your page currently has no second offer for.

The arithmetic underneath

What one of those orders is actually worth to you

The four checks are about the page. This part is about the order, and it is where the dashboard misleads, because the figure it reports is the one before anyone has been paid.

An order arrives at your listed price. Your payment processor takes a percentage and a flat fee off every one. What is left is the number your ad spend, your product cost and your shipping all come out of afterwards.

Below is the shape of that subtraction on an $80 order. Treat the rate row as an input you replace. Processing rates differ by processor, by plan and by country, and they change. Read your own off a real payout in your payments dashboard rather than trusting any number printed on a page, including this one.

One order, taken apart

Flat fee per transactionCharged once on every order, whatever the size−$0.30
What reaches you$77.38
Worked at 2.9% plus 30 cents, the rate the store in the teardown was on. Yours may differ. The flat fee bites hardest on small baskets, where the same 30 cents is 0.4% of an $80 order and 3% of a $10 one. Product cost, shipping and ad spend all come out of the $77.38.
